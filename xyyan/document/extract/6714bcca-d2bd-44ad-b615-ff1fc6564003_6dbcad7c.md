BACKGROUND: Histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) is a multifunctional plasma glycoprotein involved in the regulation of various biological processes. Decreased plasma levels of HRG at 72 h after lung transplantation have been shown to be associated with severe primary graft dysfunction and poor transplant outcomes. In this study, we examined the effect of HRG supplementation on lung ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) in mice.

METHODS: The experimental mice were divided into a Sham group and three IRI groups (n=5 each) that received phosphate-buffered saline, human serum albumin, or HRG (HRG/IRI group), respectively. The lung function, lung injury, neutrophil infiltration, cellular apoptosis, inflammatory mediators, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), translocation status of high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and hemolytic products in the injured tissue/plasma were compared among the groups.

RESULTS: The HRG/IRI group showed significantly improved oxygenation capacity and a reduced severity of lung injury as compared with the other IRI groups. The HRG/IRI group also showed

significantly less severe tissue inflammation in the reperfused lung, including a lower level of neutrophil infiltration, apoptotic cells, and neutrophil-associated inflammatory mediators than the other IRI groups. Moreover, formation of NETs and HMGB1 translocation were significantly suppressed in the HRG/IRI group as compared with the other IRI groups. A significantly greater degree of scavenging of hemolytic products was observed in the HRG/IRI group than the other IRI groups.

CONCLUSION: HRG supplementation ameliorated lung IRI in mice by suppressing inflammatory responses triggered by neutrophil-associated factors, including formation of NETs, HMGB1 translocation, and accumulation of hemolytic products.

## I ntroduction

Lung transplantation (LT) is offered as a last resort for patients with end-stage lung diseases. However, lung ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), a major cause of primary graft dysfunction (PGD) after LT, is associated with chronic lung allograft rejection and reduced survival after LT. $ ^{1} $  Lung IRI is associated with cellular stress, oxidative damage and inflammation, resulting in graft dysfunction and poor transplant outcomes. $ ^{2} $  Lung IRI involves multifaceted mechanisms, including formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) $ ^{3} $  and release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) $ ^{4} $  and hemolytic products. $ ^{5} $  Among these, HMGB1-mediated inflammatory responses have been shown to be inhibited by histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG). $ ^{6} $ 

HRG, which was first isolated from the human serum and characterized in 1972, $ ^{7} $  is a 75-kDa multifunctional plasma glycoprotein that is mainly produced in the liver and occurs relatively abundantly in the circulation. $ ^{8} $  HRG is also expressed on the surfaces of leukocytes $ ^{9} $  and maintains circulating neutrophils in a resting state to prevent vascular endothelial cells from uncontrolled activation. $ ^{10} $  In fact, plasma HRG levels are decreased in mice with sepsis, whose survival has been shown to improve with HRG supplementation. $ ^{10} $  Patients with sepsis showing decreased plasma HRG levels have also been demonstrated to show poor survival. $ ^{11} $ 

Consistent with the observations in patients with sepsis, we showed in a previous study that LT patients showing decreased plasma HRG levels at 72 h after LT developed severe PGD and showed decreased overall survival after LT. $ ^{[12]} $  Therefore, we hypothesized that HRG could offer promise as a potential therapeutic target for lung IRI. In this study, we examined the effect of HRG supplementation on lung IRI using a mouse hilar clamp model.

## Materials and methods

## Animals

Pathogen-free male C57BL/6J mice (CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) weighing 20–25 g at 8–12 weeks were utilized for the experiments. All mice were cared for in accordance with the principles of laboratory animal care formulated by the National Society for Medical Research and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals: Eighth Edition (National Academy Press, 2011). The research protocol was approved by the relevant ethics committee and institutional review board (OKU-2021661).

## Purification of HRG from human plasma

HRG was purified from human plasma (supplied by the Japanese Red Cross Society), as described previously. $ ^{[13]} $  In brief, human plasma was incubated with nickel-nitrile-triacetic acid agarose (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) for 2 h at 4 °C under gentle shaking. The gel was packed in a column and washed sequentially with 10 mM Tris-buffered saline containing 10 mM imidazole (pH 8.0) and 10 mM Tris buffer containing 1 M NaCl (pH 8.0). Human HRG was eluted with 0.5 M imidazole in 10 mM Tris-buffered saline (pH 8.0). The protein extract was further purified on a Mono Q column (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK) with an NaCl gradient. Purified human HRG was identified by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and Western blotting using human HRG-specific antibodies.

## Lung IRI model

A mouse hilar clamp model was used as the lung IRI model (Figure 1), as previously described $ ^{4} $  (see Supplemental Material for details). Animals in the Sham group (n = 5) underwent left thoracotomy without a hilar clamp and the healthy control group (n = 2) consisted of clean animals that were immediately sacrificed. Mice in the IRI groups were subjected to left lung ischemia for 1.5 h using a left hilar clamp, followed by 3 h of reperfusion. At sacrifice, arterial blood gases were measured, with oxygen delivered at a FiO $ _{2} $  of 1.0 after clamping the right pulmonary hilum for 3 min. $ ^{14} $

## Histological lung injury score analysis

Histological lung injury was evaluated in a blinded manner by two pathologists (T. O. and A. M.), who assigned scores to the specimens based on the lung injury criteria: (1) neutrophil infiltration or aggregation; (2) airway epithelial cell damage; (3) interstitial edema; (4) hyaline membrane formation; and (5) hemorrhage. $ ^{15} $  The severity of each pathological feature was assigned a score of 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4, corresponding to absent, minimal, mild, moderate, or severe injury, respectively. The total score obtained by adding the scores for the individual pathological features represented the total lung injury score.

## Analysis of the severity of apoptosis

The apoptosis severity was analyzed by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated deoxyuridine triphosphate nick-end labeling (TUNEL), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions (see Supplemental Material for details).

## Quantification of cytokines and chemokines

The concentrations of cytokines and chemokines in the plasma and lung samples were measured using Bio-Plex Pro Mouse Cytokine 23-plex Panel (Bio-Rad, CA, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

## I mmunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical analysis was performed using anti-HMGB1 pAb (#ab18256, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO) pAb (#AF3667, R&D systems, MN, USA), anti-histone H3 pAb (#ab5103, Abcam), and anti-lymphocyte antigen 6 complex locus G6D (Ly6G) mAb (#127602, Biolegend, CA, USA), as described previously $ ^{4,10} $  (see Supplemental Material for details).

NETs are defined as extracellular structures showing colocalization of MPO and histone H3, and are detected by immunofluorescence within  $ 100 \mu m $  beneath the pleura. NET-positive sites were counted in three high-power fields and their average values were determined, as described previously. $ ^{[10]} $  Cells with positive HMGB1 expression outside the nuclei were counted as cells showing HMGB1 translocation from the nuclei, and the average percentages

of these cells relative to the total number of cells in three high-power fields were calculated.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The protein levels of HMGB1 in the plasma and lung samples were evaluated using the HMGB1 ELISA Kit Exp (Shino-Test Co., Sagamihara, Japan), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

## Western blotting

Cryopreserved lung tissues were subjected to Western blotting, as previously described $ ^{4} $  (see Supplementary Material for details), using rabbit anti-HRG pAb (#GTX64492, Gene Tex, CA, USA), rabbit anti-haptoglobin (#ab1312369, Abcam), rabbit anti-hemopexin mAb (#ab150401, Abcam), and goat anti-hemoglobin (#GTX77484, Gene Tex).

## Statistical analysis

Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) was used for the statistical analyses. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t-test and comparisons among multiple groups were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by the post hoc Šidák's test for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered as denoting significance.

## Results

## Decrease of plasma HRG levels in the murine lung IRI

First, we performed preliminary experiments to determine the optimal duration of ischemia and reperfusion for obtaining appropriate mouse models of lung IRI from among different time combinations (Figure 1a). The combination of 1.5-hour ischemia and 3-hour reperfusion was identified as the optimal protocol based on significant differences observed in the following features between the groups (Figure 1b). Diffuse lung redness on gross inspection was observed in the IRI group as compared with the Sham group (Figure 2a). The lung injury scores were significantly higher in the IRI group than in the Sham group (P = 0.0085; Figure 2b, c), especially the score for hemorrhage (P = 0.0002). The oxygenation capacity was significantly worse in the IRI group than in the Sham group (P = 0.0005; Figure 2d). Notably, the plasma HRG levels were significantly lower in the IRI group than in the Sham group (P = 0.0011; Figure 2e).

## HRG supplementation ameliorated the lung IRI

Figure 3 shows the results of the treatment experiment. The HRG/IRI group showed a pinkish white appearance of the reperfused lungs as compared with the PBS/IRI and HSA/IRI groups, similar to the Sham group (Figure 3a). The HRG/IRI group showed significantly lower lung injury scores, especially the scores for neutrophil infiltration and hemorrhage, than the PBS/IRI  $ (P = 0.0028) $  and HSA/IRI  $ (P = 0.0003) $  groups (Figure 3b, c). The HRG/IRI group also showed significantly better oxygenation capacity than the PBS/IRI and HSA/IRI groups  $ (P < 0.0001 $  for both) (Figure 3d). The results of TUNEL staining performed to evaluate the severity of apoptosis as a component of tissue damage (Figure 3e) revealed significantly fewer TUNEL-positive cells in HRG/IRI group than in the PBS/IRI  $ (P = 0.0011) $  and HSA/IRI  $ (P = 0.0002) $  groups. Cytokine assay of the lung tissue revealed significantly lower levels of neutrophil-associated chemokines (Figure 3f), including C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, in the HRG/IRI group than in the PBS/IRI  $ (P = 0.0033, 0.0464, $  and 0.0012, respectively) and HSA/IRI  $ (P = 0.0200, 0.0482, $  and 0.0439, respectively) groups. Significant differences were also observed in the levels of macrophage inflammatory protein-1 $ \alpha $   $ (P = 0.0373) $ , 1 $ \beta $   $ (P = 0.0297) $ , interleukin (IL)-6  $ (P = 0.0173) $ , and IL-10  $ (P = 0.0043) $  between the HRG/IRI and PBS/IRI groups. In contrast, there was a significant difference in the level of IL-1 $ \beta $  only between the HRG/IRI and HSA/IRI groups  $ (P = 0.0247) $ . Furthermore, no significant differences in the tissue levels of tumor necrosis factor- $ \alpha $  were observed among the groups.

## HRG suppressed neutrophil-associated inflammatory responses

The results of immunohistochemical analysis revealed that, similar to the Sham group, the HRG/IRI group showed a significantly lower degree of neutrophil infiltration, which was identified as the sum of MPO- and Ly6G-positive cells, than the PBS/IRI and HSA/IRI groups (MPO, P < 0.0001 for both; Ly6G, P = 0.0014 and P < 0.0001, respectively) (Figure 4a, b). A correlation diagram between MPO- and Ly6G-positive cells is shown in Figure 4c. Neutrophil-derived MPO activity was suppressed in the HRG/IRI group as compared with the PBS/IRI and HSA/IRI groups.

Evaluation of NETs by fluorescence immunostaining (Fig. 4d) revealed that the overall number of NETs was small and that the NETs were mainly located just beneath the pleura. A significantly smaller number of NETs per unit area within 100  $ \mu $ m beneath the pleura was observed in the HRG/IRI group than in the PBS/IRI (P = 0.0082) and HSA/IRI (P = 0.0230) groups.

## HRG supplementation decreased HMGB1 translocation

Immunofluorescence staining showed that HMGB1 was localized in the nuclei in the Sham group, whereas nuclear HMGB1 was translocated to the cytoplasm and then to the extracellular spaces in the PBS/IRI and HSA/IRI groups (Figure 5a). This translocation of HMGB1 was significantly suppressed in the HRG/IRI group as compared with that in the PBS/IRI and HSA/IRI groups (P < 0.0001 for both).
