## A B S T R A C T

Aims: The ubiquitination system in hepatocytes and its direct role in antibacterial defense during sepsis remains poorly understood. This study investigated the mechanism by which the E3 ubiquitin ligase Midline 1 (Mid1) regulates the degradation of histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) during bacterial infection, and a proof-of-concept approach was proposed to block Mid1 and relieve hepatic sepsis.

Material and methods: Transcriptomic analysis of livers from a mouse cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model was used to reveal changes in the global ubiquitination and Mid1 level. Immunoprecipitation, coupled with mass spectrometry, and native PAGE, was used to assess the proteins that bind to Mid1. The therapeutic effects of Mid1 in vivo were evaluated through hepatocyte-specific siRNAs and inhibitors.

Key findings: Mid1 was significantly upregulated in hepatocytes from both CLP mice and patients with acute infection, accompanied by increased global ubiquitination. Inhibiting Mid1 in hepatocytes enhanced their antibacterial capacity and reduced infection-induced cellular damage in cellulo and in vivo experiments. Mid1 overexpression increased bacterial survival in AML12 hepatocyte cell lines. Mechanistically, Mid1 physically directly binds the HRG and targets it for ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation. Crucially, HRG, identified as a novel Mid1 substrate, is critical for antibacterial defense—blocking HRG abolished the protective effects of Mid1 knockdown. Hepatocyte-specific Mid1 siRNA and DPP4 inhibitors improved systemic outcomes and survival in septic mice.

Significance: This study highlights that the Mid1-HRG ubiquitination axis is a critical regulator of sepsis progression, suggesting a novel therapeutic strategy to augment the liver's intrinsic antibacterial activity.

### 1. Introduction

Sepsis is now defined as a dysregulated host response to infection, resulting in life-threatening organ dysfunction. The disease is critical, and the incidence and mortality rates remain high  $ [1] $ . Excessive inflammatory responses and cytokine storms during sepsis have been consistently cited as the main causes of high mortality. However, drugs targeting important inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF- $ \alpha $ , IL-1 $ \beta $ , and toll-like receptors) have not yielded satisfactory clinical results in improving the survival rates of sepsis patients  $ [2] $ . Recent studies have shown that innate immune dysfunction and acquired immunosuppression jointly contribute to multiorgan injury and sepsis-related mortality; thus, a strategy for enhancing the immune response has been proposed. However, relevant studies lack evidence from large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and are therefore not included in current sepsis treatment guidelines  $ [3] $ . The treatments recommended by guidelines mainly include resuscitation, organ support, early administration of antibiotics, and control of the infection focus. Nevertheless, specific treatment methods are still lacking.

Histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG), a hepatocyte-secreted serum protein, circulates at relatively high concentrations in human serum (100–150  $ \mu $ g/ml) and has been hailed as the “Swiss Army Knife” of serum proteins owing to its multifunctional properties [4]. During sepsis, the serum HRG concentration decreases [5] and is a reliable biomarker for determining the presence of bacterial infection and the severity of the disease in patients [6]. In the context of infection and inflammation, HRG has diverse protective effects [7], including promoting macrophage phagocytosis [8], restraining the excessive activation of neutrophils [9], inhibiting the formation of insoluble immune complexes (IIC) [10], and neutralizing endotoxins [11]. HRG exerts direct antibacterial effects by disrupting bacterial cell wall integrity in gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa) and gram-positive bacteria (Enterococcus faecalis and Staphylococcus aureus) [12]. Exogenous HRG supplementation (20 mg/kg) significantly improves survival rates in septic mice [9], highlighting its therapeutic potential. Despite these promising findings, large-scale production of medical-grade HRGs remains economically unfeasible, preventing the clinical translation of “exogenous HRG supplementation therapy” to date. The regulatory mechanisms of HRG in hepatocytes during sepsis require further investigation.

Mid1, a RING finger family E3 ubiquitin ligase, plays a pivotal role in diverse disease processes. It is involved in multiple contexts, including asthma (in bronchial epithelial cells) [13], esophagitis (in the esophageal epithelium) [14], and sepsis-induced lung injury (in postcapillary venous endothelial cells) [15]. In spinal cord injury, the inhibition of MID1 transcription promotes macrophage M2 polarization, which in turn favors the reconstruction of the neural stem cell microenvironment [16]. In multiple sclerosis models, Mid1 promotes effector T cell migration to the central nervous system by upregulating the mTOR/microtubule pathway [17]. Phosphatase 2Ac (PP2Ac) and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) serve as substrates for Mid1-mediated ubiquitination. Ubiquitin-mediated degradation of IRF3 suppresses type I interferon (IFN-I) production and impairs antiviral responses [18]. However, little is known regarding the role of hepatocyte Mid1 in septic liver injury.

In this study, we identified a significant increase in total ubiquitination levels and Mid1 expression in septic liver tissues. Hepatocyte-specific Mid1 knockdown attenuated hepatocyte damage and enhanced bacterial clearance both in cellulo and in vivo. We now demonstrated for the first time that HRG is a novel substrate of Mid1-mediated ubiquitination. Therapeutically, hepatocyte-targeted Mid1 siRNA and an oral DPP4 inhibitor effectively suppressed hepatic Mid1 expression. This intervention elevated serum HRG levels, improved liver function, and enhanced survival in septic mice. In summary, these findings reveal that the Mid1-HRG axis is as a novel regulator of liver sepsis progression, suggesting a crucial target for sepsis therapies.

### 2. Material and methods

#### 2.1. Bacterial strains and culture conditions

The E. coli and MRSA (ATCC 43300) strains were cultured overnight in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium at  $ 37\ ^{\circ}C $  with shaking at 200 rpm. The following morning, they reached the logarithmic growth phase. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600), where an OD600 of 1.0 corresponded to approximately  $ 1\times10^{9} $  colony-forming units (CFU)/ml for E. coli [19] and an OD600 of 0.5 to approximately  $ 1.5\times10^{8} $  CFU/ml for MRSA [20]. The bacterial suspensions were then diluted to the required concentrations for subsequent in vivo (animal model) and in cellulo (cell model) experiments.

#### 2.2. Animals

Male C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Charles River Laboratory (Beijing, China) and housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at the Animal Experiment Center of the Army Medical Center (Chongqing, China). The mice were maintained under controlled conditions (22–25 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle) with adequate ventilation and free access to food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University (approval no. AMUWEC20247132) and conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

#### 2.3. Human liver tissue samples

Healthy human liver tissue samples were obtained from macroscopically normal residual tissue of patients undergoing resection for benign liver diseases. Infected liver tissue samples were obtained from patients diagnosed with acute cholangitis, a condition resulting from the combination of bacterial infection and biliary obstruction  $ [21] $ . This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Army Medical Center (approval no. YYLS2025038). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The donors consented to the pseudonymized use of their tissue samples and clinical data (sex, age, diagnosis) for research and publication purposes. No compensation was provided for participation.

#### 2.4. Mouse sepsis and peritonitis models

Cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model: Following laparotomy, the cecum was exposed, and the fecal contents were concentrated in the distal cecum. The cecum was ligated and punctured approximately 1.2 cm from the distal end, allowing a small amount of fecal material to extrude before being returned to the abdominal cavity. The incision was then sutured.

LPS-induced peritonitis model: Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was dissolved in sterile saline and administered intraperitoneally (10 mg/kg). After 12 or 24 h, the mice were euthanized, and specimens were collected for further analysis.

Mouse serum was collected and analyzed via an automated biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter) to measure the following parameters. Liver function markers included alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT). Kidney function marker: Creatinine (Crea). Hematological parameter: Platelet count (PLT). HRG levels were quantified by immunoblotting and ELISA (RayBiotech, Cat#Q9ESB3).

#### 2.5. Histopathological analysis

Mouse liver samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. For morphological assessment, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed.

Immunohistochemistry was performed using a two-step universal kit (ZSGB-BIO, Cat#PV-8000). Briefly, the sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and treated with PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100) for 10 min, followed by antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections with a peroxidase inhibitor at room temperature (RT) for 10 min. An anti-Mid1 (Abclonal, Cat#A7291) antibody was applied, and the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, an enzyme-linked sheep anti-mouse/rabbit IgG polymer was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. After another PBS wash, the sections were developed with DAB chromogen, counterstained with hematoxylin, and differentiated until the nuclei appeared blue. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for microscopic analysis.

For immunofluorescence staining, endogenous peroxidases were blocked as described for IHC. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-Mid1 rabbit antibody (ABclonal, Cat#A7291), anti-CD3 rabbit antibody (Abcam, Cat#ab16669), anti-CD15 rabbit antibody (Abcam, ab15377), anti-CD68 rabbit antibody (Abcam, Cat#ab125212), and anti-HRG mouse antibody (Santa Cruz, Cat#sc-398,239). The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, washed with PBS, and then incubated with the following secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488-labelled anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher, Cat#A-21202) or Alexa Fluor 647-labelled anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher, Cat#A-21245). After 2 h at RT, the nuclei were stained with DAPI (5 min, RT). The sections were washed, mounted with an anti-fade medium, and coverslipped. The fluorescence signals were detected via an OLYMPUS superresolution microscope system.

#### 2.6. Isolation of primary hepatocytes

Primary hepatocytes were isolated using an established protocol  $ [22] $ . Briefly, the liver tissue was digested to create a hepatocyte suspension, which was then filtered through a 70  $ \mu $ m cell strainer. The filtrate was centrifuged at  $ 50 \times g $  for 2 min at 4  $ {}^{\circ} $ C to pellet the hepatocytes. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in the appropriate medium for subsequent experiments.

#### 2.7. Cell culture and transfection

The following cell lines were cultured under standard conditions (37  $ {}^{\circ} $ C, 5 %CO $ _{2} $ , humidified atmosphere): AML12 (Alpha mouse liver 12; Procell Life Science Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) in AML12-specific medium, Kupffer macrophages in RPMI-1640 medium, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) and hepatic stellate cells (JS1) in DMEM-H medium. All culture media were supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 100 U/ml penicillin-streptomycin.

AML12 cells were transfected with lentiviral vectors for delivery via gene-editing systems. Murine Mid1 knockdown: Cells were transduced with a siRNA-containing lentivirus (target sequence: GACCGATGACCAGTTAATCTGTGTTTTTT) constructed by Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Murine Mid1 overexpression: Mid1 transcript variant 3 sequence from NCBI GenBank (NM_001290504.1) was cloned and inserted into lentiviral vector particles (GenePharma Technology Co., Ltd.). The cells were transduced with a Mid1-overexpressing lentivirus. The optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) was determined through preliminary screening. Stable cell lines were established according to the manufacturers' protocols and subsequently validated.

#### 2.8. In cellulo model

An in cellulo coculture system was established using AML12 hepatocytes, Kupffer macrophages, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), and hepatic stellate cells (JS1). The cells were treated with or without E. coli (MOI of 10:1), LPS (1  $ \mu $ g/ml; Sigma, Cat#L5293), TAK243 (1  $ \mu $ M; MCE, Cat#HY-100487), CHX (10  $ \mu $ g/ml; MCE, Cat#HY-12320), or MG132 (10  $ \mu $ M; MCE, Cat#HY-13259). Notably, antibiotics were excluded from the medium during the bacterial challenge experiments. After treatment, the cells were harvested for RT-qPCR and Immunoblot analysis.

#### 2.9. Heparin-bacterial competition assay for HRG binding

The method employed for the heparin-bacterial competition assay to evaluate HRG binding was adapted from the literature  $ [23] $ . In summary, AML12 cells  $ (2 \times 10^{6}) $  were cultured in antibiotic-free medium for 12 h and subsequently infected with E. coli  $ (1.5 \times 10^{8} $  CFU) per well. Heparin (0, 10, 100, 200 and 400  $ \mu $ g/ml) was then added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Following incubation, the cells were centrifuged at  $ 300 \times g $  for 5 min, and the supernatants were collected. These supernatants were utilized directly for soluble protein analysis, while the cell pellets were lysed in RIPA buffer. Equal volumes of each supernatant along with equivalent amounts of proteins from the pellet samples were subjected to immunoblotting analysis.

#### 2.10. Immunoprecipitation

Cultured cells were lysed on ice for 10 min in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, after which the lysate was centrifuged. The total protein in the supernatant was collected, quantified and incubated with an anti-Mid1 antibody (Thermo Scientific, Cat# PA5–36305 and Abclonal, Cat# A7291), an anti-ubiquitin antibody (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-8017), and the corresponding isotype IgG (CST, Cat# 3900). A Pierce Coimmunoprecipitation Kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat# 88828) was used according to the manufacturer's protocol, followed by immunoblot analysis.

#### 2.11. Native PAGE protocol

The cell samples were collected and lysed with a non-denaturing lysis buffer (Sangon Biotech, Cat# C51001). The lysate was centrifuged to pellet the cellular debris, and the supernatant was mixed with native PAGE protein loading buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0016) to prepare the samples. An 8 % SDS-free acrylamide gel was used for separation. Electrophoresis was performed at a low temperature using Tris-Glycine electrophoresis buffer (pH 8.8), followed by immunoblot analysis. In parallel, the gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Beyotime, Cat# P0003) to visualize the protein bands.

#### 2.12. Immunoblot analysis

Tissue samples and cultured cells (particularly diluted mouse serum and cultured supernatant for HRG analysis) were lysed via RIPA buffer (CST, #9806) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (CST, Cat#5872). The lysates were centrifuged for clarification, and the supernatants were collected for immunoblotting. Equal amounts of total protein were loaded onto Tris-glycine extended stain-free polyacrylamide gels. Electrophoresis was performed to separate the proteins under denaturing conditions. Total protein, measured using a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad), serve as a stain-free loading control for normalization. After the proteins were transferred to a PVDF or nitrocellulose membrane, the membrane was blocked to prevent nonspecific binding of antibodies binding prior to immunoblotting. The PVDF membranes were incubated with the following primary antibodies: (HRG (ABclonal, Cat# A8431), Mid1 (Thermo Scientific, Cat# PA5-36305), Capza1 (ABclonal, Cat# A3776), PP2Aca (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-80,665), total ubiquitination (PTMBIO, Cat# PTM-5798), ubiquitin (ABclonal, Cat# A19686), GAPDH (CST, Cat# 5174),  $ \beta $ -actin

(CST, Cat# 4967), Albumin (ABclonal, Cat# A24161), and HNF4α (Bioyuntime, Cat# AF7122). Then, secondary antibodies and chemiluminescent detection protocols were used as previously described. Immunoreactive protein bands were visualized via the ChemiDoc Touch System (Bio-Rad, USA).

#### 2.13. RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from the samples using TRIzol reagent, following the manufacturer's protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized via the HiScript II One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Vazyme, Cat# RR014BP611). Gene expression was analyzed via quantitative PCR (qPCR) with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat# Q321) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). GAPDH served as the internal control, and relative gene expression levels were calculated via the  $ 2 - \Delta\Delta Ct $  method. The mRNA expression of the target gene was compared to that of the control group, following previously established protocols.

The sequences of primers used were as follows:

mMid1F: CCAGGTCCCTAATTCCCGAAATCA;

mMid1R: GGGTTGGGAGCTGTTAGGTAATC.

mGAPDH F: CAGTGGCAAAGTGGAGATTGTTG;

mGAPDH R: TCGCTCCTGGAAGATGGTGAT.

hMID1F: CTGACCTGCCCTATTTGTCTG;

hMID1R: GCACAGTGTGATACTAGGATGC.

#### 2.14. Flow cytometry

A PE Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with 7-AAD (BioLegend, Cat# 640934) was used to identify apoptotic and necrotic cells. After 12 h of coculture with E. coli, the cells were collected and stained according to the manufacturer's protocol, followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature. The stained cells were then analyzed via an ACEA NovoCyte $ ^{TM} $  flow cytometer, and the data were processed with FlowJo V10.3 software.

#### 2.15. Viral vector construction, siRNA preparation and administration

An adenoviral vector was constructed via the GV698 promoter. The sequence for Mid1 was: GACCGATGACCAGTTAATCTGTGTTTTTT. For in vivo delivery, 100  $ \mu $ l of adeno-associated virus (AAV;  $ 2 \times 10^{12} $  viral particles/ml) was administered intravenously, with AAV-NC serving as the negative control. The infection model was successfully established three weeks after injection.

The following Cy3-labelled siRNAs were synthesized by GenePharma Technology Co. (Shanghai, China): Mid1-targeting siRNA (mid1-siRNA; antisense: 5'-AUAAGAUUGGUGAGAUUACUC-3'); scrambled control siRNA (siN. C; antisense: 5'-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3'). siRNAs were administered intravenously (60  $ \mu $ g per 20 g body weight) 12 h prior to CLP induction.

#### 2.16. Bacterial viability analysis

To assess bacterial viability, the cells were cultured in media without antibiotics and infected with MRSA or E. coli at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 2 for 18 h. Bacterial viability was then evaluated via the LIVE/DEAD Bacterial Viability Kit (Invitrogen, Cat# L7012), following the manufacturer's protocol (live bacteria: green fluorescence; dead bacteria: red fluorescence). The fluorescence signals were visualized and quantified via an Olympus superresolution microscope system.

#### 2.17. Transcriptomic analyses

Total RNA was extracted from the livers of CLP  $ (n=5) $ , Sham  $ (n=3) $ , CLP_NC  $ (n=3) $ , and CLP_shMid1  $ (n=3) $  mice for CLP vs Sham and CLP_NC vs CLP_shMid1 analysis. RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) with a PE150 read length following the manufacturer's standard protocol. Low-quality reads, adapter-contaminated reads, and reads with  $ >10\% $  unknown bases were filtered out to obtain clean reads. Clean reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) via HISAT2 (v2.2.1), and gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM values. DEGs between groups were identified via DESeq2 (v1.34.0) with the following cutoffs:  $ \log_{2}(\text{fold change}) > 2 $  and adjusted p value <0.05. The functional annotation of the DEGs was performed via Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, with significance defined as an adjusted p value <0.05. The results were visualized via ggplot2 (v3.4.4) in R software (v4.2.3).

#### 2.18. Immunoprecipitation coupled mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analysis

The IP protocol is described in section 2.10. The eluted proteins were reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol, alkylated with 20 mM iodoacetamide, and digested with trypsin at 37 °C overnight. Tryptic peptides were analyzed via a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer coupled with an Easy-nLC 1200 ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system. The MS1 scan range was 350–1800 m/z; MS2 scans were performed with higher-energy collisional dissociation. Peptide and protein identification was performed via MaxQuant (v1.6) against the UniProt mouse protein database. The false discovery rate (FDR) for peptide and protein identification was set to <1 %. For protein quantification, the label-free quantification (LFQ) intensity was used, and interacting proteins of Mid1 were screened with the following cutoff: LFQ intensity ratio > 2 and p value <0.05.

#### 2.19. GalNAc-siMid1

GalNAc-siMid1 was designed on the basis of Mid1 mRNA (GenBank: NM_001290504.1). Sense sequences: 5'-GUAAUCUCACCAAUCUUAU-3'; antisense sequences: 5'-AUAAGA UUGGUGAGAUUACUC-3'. siRNA strands were chemically synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China) with 2'-O-methyl modification at the 3'-terminal nucleotides for nuclease resistance. Duplexes were purified by HPLC, and their integrity was verified via 15% urea-PAGE. GalNAc was conjugated to the 3'-end of the siRNA sense strand via a 6-carbon spacer phosphorothioate (PS) linker, enabling binding to the hepatocyte asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR).

#### 2.20. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed unpaired Student's t-tests or one-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons test, based on the data distribution and experimental design. Specific statistical tests for each dataset are detailed in the corresponding figure legends. The experiments were repeated at least three times. Data processing was performed via GraphPad Prism 9.1 and SPSS 25.0 for analysis and visualization. A  $ *p^{*}<0.05 $  was considered statistically significant.

### 3. Results

3.1. E3 ubiquitin ligase Mid1 in the liver is elevated in both a mouse model of sepsis and infected patients

To investigate changes in ubiquitination in the livers of septic mice, we first assessed global ubiquitination levels. We found that livers from cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) mice exhibited significantly greater ubiquitination than did those from sham controls (Fig. 1A). The intravenous administration of the ubiquitination inhibitor TAK-243 decreased total ubiquitination by immunoblotting (Fig. 1B, S1A) and reduced the mRNA expression levels of the inflammatory factors TNF- $ \alpha $ .

IL-1 $ \beta $  and Tnfaip3 (TNF $ \alpha $ -induced protein 3) according to RT-qPCR (Fig. S1B) in the livers of CLP mice but did not alleviate liver injury (Fig. S1C) or improve survival rates (Fig. 1C). To determine whether a specific ubiquitin ligase plays a dominant role, we performed transcriptomic analysis of CLP and sham mouse livers. A total of 393 DEGs were enriched in inflammatory response, immune regulation, and hepatocyte function pathways (Fig. 1D; Fig. S1D, E). Focusing on E3 ubiquitin ligases from the UBIBROWSER database, we identified three candidate E3 ubiquitin ligases (mid1, Pdzrn3, and Rnf180) (Fig. S1F). The heatmap shows DEGs of genes of partially interested in the livers of the Sham and CLP groups (Fig. 1E). A chord diagram showing the functional enrichment of these DEGs is shown in Fig. 1F. These results suggested that Mid1 presented the highest expression and pathway enrichment. Consistently, mid1 mRNA and protein (Fig. 1G-H) expression levels in the liver were elevated in both the LPS-induced sepsis model and the CLP-induced sepsis model. Moreover, liver samples from patients with acute cholangitis also presented increased MID1 mRNA (Fig. 1I) and protein expression (Fig. 1J-K, S1G). These findings suggest that mid1 may play a critical role in infection-related liver pathology.

#### 3.2. Mid1 expression is predominantly elevated in hepatocytes from septic liver tissue

Although hepatocytes constitute the majority of liver cells, we sought to specifically determine whether Mid1 upregulation during sepsis occurs primarily in these cells. To investigate this, we isolated hepatocytes from CLP mice via gradient centrifugation, and separated them into hepatocyte and nonhepatocyte fractions (Fig. 2A). Successful separation was confirmed by analyzing hepatocyte-specific and inflammatory markers (Fig. 2B). The Mid1 mRNA and protein (Fig. 2B-C) levels were significantly greater in hepatocytes than in nonhepatocyte cells via quantitative analysis. We further validated these findings in different cell lines (not only primary hepatocytes) treated with LPS or infected with E. coli for 12 h, including hepatocytes (AML12), Kupffer cells (macrophages), liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), and hepatic stellate cells (JS1), and the Mid1 protein (Fig. 2D) and mRNA (Fig. 2E) expression levels were predominantly elevated in hepatocytes. Primary hepatocytes were isolated from wild-type C57BL/6 J mice via in situ perfusion (Fig. S2A) and, after 24 h of culture, were treated with LPS or infected with E. coli for 12 h. Subsequent analyses revealed a differential response. While E. coli infection robustly increased both Mid1 mRNA (Fig. 2F) and protein levels (Fig. 2G-I); LPS stimulation resulted in only a marginal upregulation compared to the control. This disparity is likely attributable to the distinct signaling mechanisms engaged by purified LPS (primarily via TLR4) versus whole E. coli, which activates a broader repertoire of pattern recognition receptors. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Mid1 is upregulated in hepatocytes during bacterial infection, with a notably stronger induction by whole bacteria than by LPS alone.

#### 3.3. Mid1 inhibition enhances hepatocyte antibacterial activity and reduces the degree of cellular damage induced by infection

To investigate the role of Mid1 in hepatocytes during infection, we generated murine Mid1-knockdown (shMid1) and Mid1-overexpressing (Lv-Mid1) AML12 hepatocyte cell lines, the efficiency of which was confirmed via RT-qPCR (Fig. 3A) and immunoblot analysis (Fig. 3B, C). Notably, under unstimulated conditions with low basal Mid1 expression, shRNA-mediated knockdown only slightly reduced Mid1 protein levels. In contrast, upon stimulation, the shRNA directly abrogated the robust, inducible synthesis of Mid1, resulting in a far more pronounced reduction in Mid1 abundance (Fig. 3B). Correspondingly, Lv-Mid1 cells exhibited further elevation of Mid1 protein levels following stimulation with LPS or E. coli (Fig. 3C). These observations led us to hypothesize that LPS or E. coli signaling either inhibits a pathway responsible for Mid1 degradation or promotes post-translational modifications that enhance Mid1 stability, thereby driving its accumulation independent of its transcriptional regulation. These data suggest that Mid1 upregulation upon stimulation is achieved through a combination of transcriptional induction and potential protein stabilization. Supernatants from shMid1 cells collected 12 h post-infection (E. coli or MRSA) yielded significantly fewer bacterial colonies than shNC controls did in plating assays, whereas Lv-Mid1 supernatants produced more colonies (Fig. 3D, E). Consistent with these findings, live/dead bacterial staining revealed fewer live bacteria and a higher dead-to-live bacteria ratio in shMid1 supernatants but a lower ratio in the Lv-Mid1 groups (Fig. 3F, G). These results demonstrated that Mid1 suppression bolsters the antibacterial capacity of hepatocytes. Consistent with these findings, shMid1 AML12 hepatocytes presented fewer bacteria, but Mid1 overexpression (Lv-Mid1) AML12 hepatocytes presented more bacteria after incubation with E. coli for 24 h, according to microscopy images (Fig. 3H) and bacterial growth curve analysis (Fig. 3I). Trypan blue staining revealed significantly fewer dead cells in the shMid1 groups than in the control or Lv-mid1 groups post-infection (Fig. 3H, J). Flow cytometry (annexin V-PE/7-AAD) confirmed these results (Fig. 3K). These results indicate that Mid1 inhibition decreases the number of bacterial colonies and mitigates infection-induced hepatocyte damage in cells.

#### 3.4. Hepatocyte-specific Mid1 knockdown improves organ status and survival in septic mice

To investigate the role of Mid1 in sepsis, we performed in vivo experiments using a liver-targeted adeno-associated virus (AAV) to construct a hepatocyte-specific Mid1 knockdown mouse model (Mid1 $ ^{hep-KD} $ ) (Fig. 4A). RT-qPCR confirmed the knockdown efficiency (Fig. 4B), and fluorescence imaging of multiple organs demonstrated AAV-specific liver tropism (Fig. 4C). Compared with the AAV-NC group, the Mid1 $ ^{hep-KD} $  group presented a significantly greater survival rate after cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) or intraperitoneal E. coli infection (Fig. 4D). Additionally, the AST (aspartate aminotransferase), Cr (creatinine) and LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) activities were significantly lower in the Mid1 $ ^{hep-KD} $  group than in the control group, but the number of PLTs (platelets) was significantly greater (Fig. 4E, Fig. S2B). Blood PLTs are key mediators of inflammation and infection [24]. These results suggested that Mid1 blockade alleviated liver and kidney injury induced by CLP. Notably, bacterial loads in major organs were markedly reduced in Mid1 $ ^{hep-KD} $  mice (Fig. 4F, G).

Histopathological analysis of liver tissue revealed extensive immune cell infiltration in both the Mid1 $ ^{hep-KD} $  and AAV-NC groups after CLP or E. coli challenge. However, compared with control mice, Mid1 $ ^{hep-KD} $  group mice presented significantly smaller areas of hepatocyte degeneration and necrosis (orange arrows) and less immune cell infiltrates (green arrows) in H&E-stained liver sections (Fig. 4H) and a lower histological activity index (Fig. 4I). We further assessed inflammatory responses in peritoneal macrophages. The mRNA expression levels of proinflammatory factors (TNF $ \alpha $ , IL-1 $ \beta $  and TGF $ \beta $ ) and the key transcription factor STAT1 were significantly decreased after CLP.
