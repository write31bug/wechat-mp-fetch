# Histidine-Rich Glycoprotein Modulates the Toxic Effects of High-Dose Polyphosphate in Mice

Rida A. Malik $ ^{ID} $ , Ji Zhou $ ^{ID} $ , James C. Fredenburgh $ ^{ID} $ , Jeff Crosby, Alexey S. Revenko $ ^{ID} $ , Jeff S. Healey $ ^{ID} $ , Jeffrey I. Weitz $ ^{ID} $ 

BACKGROUND: Polyphosphate (polyP), a procoagulant released from platelets, activates coagulation via the contact system and modulates cardiomyocyte viability. High-dose intravenous polyP is lethal in mice, presumably because of thrombosis. Previously, we showed that HRG (histidine-rich glycoprotein) binds polyP and attenuates its procoagulant effects. In this study, we investigated the mechanisms responsible for the lethality of intravenous polyP in mice and the impact of HRG on this process.

METHODS: The survival of wild-type or HRG-deficient mice given intravenous synthetic or platelet-derived polyP in doses up to 50 mg/kg or saline was compared. To determine the contribution of thrombosis, the effect of FXII (factor XII) knockdown or enoxaparin on polyP-induced fibrin deposition in the lungs was examined. To assess cardiotoxicity, the ECG was continuously monitored, the levels of troponin I and the myocardial band of creatine kinase were quantified, and the viability of a cultured murine cardiomyocyte cell line exposed to polyP in the absence or presence of HRG was determined.

RESULTS: In HRG-deficient mice, polyP was lethal at 30 mg/kg, whereas it was lethal in wild-type mice at 50 mg/kg. Although FXII knockdown or enoxaparin administration attenuated polyP-induced fibrin deposition in the lungs, neither affected mortality. PolyP induced dose-dependent ECG abnormalities, including heart block and ST-segment changes, and increased the levels of troponin and myocardial band of creatine kinase, effects that were more pronounced in HRG-deficient mice than in wild-type mice and were attenuated when HRG-deficient mice were given supplemental HRG. Consistent with its cardiotoxicity, polyP reduced the viability of cultured cardiomyocytes in a dose-dependent manner, an effect attenuated with supplemental HRG.

CONCLUSIONS: High-dose intravenous polyP is cardiotoxic in mice, and HRG modulates this effect.

Inorganic polyphosphates (polyPs) are linear polymers of phosphate residues linked by high-energy phosphoanhydride bonds. $ ^{1} $  Each phosphate residue carries a monovalent negative charge, rendering polyP highly anionic at physiological pH. $ ^{2} $  PolyP is ubiquitous and varies in polymer length from a few to several thousand phosphate units depending on the organism and the tissue in which it is synthesized. $ ^{2} $  In mammals, polyP has been implicated in blood coagulation, innate immunity, inflammation, cancer metastasis, cell proliferation, and cell death. $ ^{3,4} $  Therefore, polyP has myriad functions.

In humans, large amounts of polyP are found in the dense granules of platelets. $ ^{5,6} $  Platelet polyP, which is 60 to 100 phosphate units in length, is released when platelets are activated. $ ^{5,6} $  Platelet polyP promotes coagulation by inducing FXII (factor XII) autoactivation, $ ^{7-10} $  promoting FV (factor V) and FXI (factor XI) activation by thrombin, $ ^{11-13} $  and enhancing the stability of fibrin clots. $ ^{14-16} $  Therefore, platelet polyP appears to be an important physiological activator of coagulation.

HRG (histidine-rich glycoprotein) is a 75-kDa glycoprotein that is synthesized in the liver and that circulates in plasma at 1 to 3  $ \mu $ mol/L. $ ^{24} $  HRG modulates the prothrombotic effect of intraperitoneal polyP injection in mice because compared with wild-type mice, HRG-deficient (HRG $ ^{-/-} $ ) mice exhibit increased thrombin generation and fibrin deposition, effects abolished with HRG supplementation. $ ^{25} $  There are at least 2 mechanisms by which HRG attenuates the prothrombotic effects of intraperitoneal polyP. First, HRG binds polyP and neutralizes its procoagulant activities. $ ^{25} $  Second, HRG binds FXIIa (activated FXII) and attenuates its capacity to promote autoactivation and to activate FXII. $ ^{25-27} $  The relative importance of these 2 mechanisms is uncertain.

When given by intravenous injection to mice, concentrations of polyP up to 300 mg/kg cause death within minutes. $ ^{[7,28,29]} $  Mortality was attributed to overwhelming thrombosis, and consequently, high-dose intravenous polyP infusion is often used as a trigger of thrombosis in animal models. ## METHODS

The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article (and the Supplemental Material).

## Reagents and Isolation of Platelet-Derived polyP

The source of reagents and the methods for the isolation of platelet-derived polyP, in vivo studies, histology, and cell culture are available in the Major Resources Table in the Supplemental Material. Synthetic short-chain polyP, which was generously provided by Dr Thomas Staffel (70.5 CAREPHOS 244; BK Giulini, GmbH, Ladenburg, Germany), was dissolved in 20 mmol/L HEPES, pH 7.4, for the in vitro studies and in 0.9% sterile saline for the in vivo studies without added calcium. PolyP was isolated from platelets as described in the Supplemental Material. The various polyP preparations were subjected to TBE (tris-boric acid)-urea gel analysis and their capacities to promote FXII autoactivation were compared. On TBE-urea gel analysis, the size distribution of platelet-derived polyP was like that of synthetic short-chain polyP from BK Giulini and commercially available polyP-100 from Kerafast (Boston, MA), whereas longer polyP polymers were evident in synthetic long-chain polyP from BK Giulini (Figure S1A). All the polyP preparations promoted FXII autoactivation to a similar extent (Figure S1B). PolyP concentrations used in this study are given in terms of phosphate monomer  $ \left(\mathrm{NaPO}_{3}\right) $  concentration as described previously. $ ^{3,6} $ 

## Mice

HRG $ ^{-/-} $  mice were maintained on a pure C57BL/6N background (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, MA) after >10

generations of backcrossing. $ ^{[25,30,31]} $  Wild-type C57BL/6J mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, MA) and were bred in-house for at least 5 generations. Mice were housed in Micro-Isolator cages exposed to a 12-hour constant light-dark cycle and were given free access to food and water. Weight- and age-matched female mice were used for all the experiments. Blood was collected, and serum and plasma were prepared as described in the Supplemental Material. All animal utilization protocols were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Board at McMaster University, and studies were performed according to the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines.

## I ntravenous polyP Injection in Mice

HRG $ ^{-/-} $  and wild-type mice were anesthetized using a combination of oxygen (0.5 L/min) and isoflurane (1%–4%) delivered by face mask. After the skin was aseptically prepared, the jugular vein was exposed via a midline incision in the neck and a cannulus (polyethylene tubing) was inserted and secured in place with a suture. Synthetic or platelet-derived polyP (10–50 mg/kg in saline) or an equivalent volume of saline was given intravenously via an infusion pump (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA) at a rate of 2  $ \mu $ L/min per g. Both the synthetic and platelet-derived polyP preparations tested negative for endotoxin activity in a screening assay (Pierce Endotoxin Chromogenic Kit from Thermo Fisher). For comparison purposes, an intravenous dose of 50 mg/kg polyP produces a circulating polyP concentration of about 7 mmol/L assuming a 1-mL blood volume for a 25-g mouse. The plasma and tissue samples were collected 30 minutes after treatment with either saline or polyP or at the time of death if it occurred earlier. Mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation, blood was collected via puncture of the inferior vena cava, and plasma or serum was prepared as detailed in the Supplemental Material (Figure S2). The lungs, liver, heart, and kidneys were removed for histological analysis.

## Effects of HRG Supplementation, FXII Knockdown, or Treatment With Enoxaparin

To investigate the potential protective effects of HRG and the contribution of FXII in this model, some HRG $ ^{-/-} $  mice were given a retro-orbital injection of 6 mg/kg of human HRG before the intravenous administration of 30 mg/kg polyP. This dose of supplemental human HRG was chosen because it raises the plasma HRG concentration to 1.5 to 2  $ \mu $ mol/L, which is similar to that in wild-type mice. $ ^{31} $ 

To determine whether thrombosis was responsible for the toxicity of polyP, we examined the effect of FXII knockdown or enoxaparin. The levels of FXII in wild-type or  $ HRG^{-/-} $ mice were knocked down with a liver-directed, mouse-specific FXII-directed ASO generously provided by Ionis Pharmaceuticals (Carlsbad, CA). $ ^{25} $  The FXII ASO was administered subcutaneously at a dose of 20 mg/kg twice weekly for 4 weeks (Figure S3), and the extent of knockdown was assessed by comparing hepatic mRNA and plasma FXII levels (Figure S4) before and after ASO administration as described previously. $ ^{25} $  Treatment with FXII ASO significantly reduced FXII levels by over 99% (P<0.001; Figure S4). Enoxaparin (10 mg/kg) was given via the retro-orbital vein 30 minutes before the intravenous injection of 50 mg/kg polyP or an equivalent volume of saline. This dose of enoxaparin produced plasma anti-Xa and anti-IIa levels of  $ 1.2\pm0.2 $  and  $ 0.5\pm0.1 $  U/mL, respectively (Figure S5), consistent with the reported anti-Xa/anti-IIa ratio of enoxaparin of about 3 to  $ 1^{32,33} $ . The anti-Xa level was within the reported therapeutic range of 0.6 to 1.0 IU/mL. $ ^{32,33} $ 

## ECG Monitoring

To assess for cardiotoxicity, the ECG was continuously monitored after induction of anesthesia using an animal BioAmp recorder (AD Instruments) and subdermal electrodes (AD Instruments, Sydney, Australia) inserted into the right hind foot, left chest/breast, and right neck-shoulder area. ECG tracings were recorded starting 2 minutes before jugular vein cannulation and continuing until 30 minutes after polyP or saline injection or until death. The ECG tracings were analyzed by 3 investigators (R.A.M, J.Z., and J.S.H., an electrophysiologist), who were blinded to treatment allocation, using a combination of visual inspection and LabChart 8 software (AD Instruments, Dunedin, New Zealand).

## Levels of Biomarkers

Serum levels of murine troponin I and CK-MB were quantified using immunoassays (Biomatik, Cambridge, Ontario, Canada) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Plasma creatinine levels were quantified using a kit purchased from Crystal Chem (Elk Grove Village, IL). Plasma thrombin-antithrombin (TAT) complex levels were quantified by immunoassay (Affinity Biologicals, Ancaster, Ontario, Canada) as described previously. $ ^{25} $ 

## Effects of polyP on Cultured Cells

Mouse cardiomyocyte HL-1 and HEK293 cell line were cultured to about 60% confluence in 8-well chambers coated with fibronectin (10  $ \mu $ g/mL per well). The cells were exposed to polyP in concentrations up to 42 mmol/L or buffer for up to 24 hours. Cell cytotoxicity was assessed by measuring the levels of LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) in the culture medium using a commercial kit (Thermo Fisher, Grand Island, NY). The effect of polyP on cell structure was assessed by staining for actin filaments (F-actin) after the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS, and blocked by incubation with 1% BSA in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. Actin filaments were stained with 1X Phallodin-iFluor-488 (Cayman Chemicals, Ann Arbor, MI), and the nuclei were stained with DAPI (4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). The cells were imaged using the Olympus BX41 fluorescence microscope equipped with a DP72 camera (Center Valley, PA), and images were assessed using the ImageJ software, version 1.48 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

## Statistical Analysis

All results are presented as mean $ \pm $ SD. Unless otherwise stated, experiments were performed at least 3 $ \times $ . The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test for normality, and the significance of differences was determined by 1- or 2-way ANOVA followed by the Holm-Sidak or Tukey comparison test. SigmaPlot, version

11 (Systat Software, San Jose, CA), and GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) were used to create graphs, and IBM SPSS Statistics software (Armonk, NY) was used to perform the statistical analyses. For all analyses, P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## RESULTS

## I ntravenous polyP Is Lethal in Wild-Type and HRG $ ^{-/-} $  Mice

Wild-type or  $ HRG^{-/-} $  mice were given intravenous synthetic polyP in doses up to 50 mg/kg or an equivalent volume of saline. With a synthetic polyP dose of 50 mg/kg, all the wild-type and  $ HRG^{-/-} $  mice died within 5 minutes, whereas all the saline control mice survived (Figure 1A and 1B). With a synthetic polyP dose of 30 mg/kg, 7 of 8 wild-type mice survived, whereas all the  $ HRG^{-/-} $  mice died with a significantly  $ (P<0.001) $  shorter mean survival time of  $ 5\pm2 $  minutes. At 50 mg/kg, platelet-derived and synthetic polyP had similar effects on survival times (Figure S6). Consequently, synthetic polyP was used for subsequent studies.

## Procoagulant and Prothrombotic Effects of polyP Are Greater in HRG $ ^{-/-} $  Mice Than in Wild-Type Mice

Blood was collected from wild-type and  $ HRG^{-/-} $  mice before and 30 minutes after they were given 50 mg/kg of polyP or saline or at the time of death, whichever came sooner, and plasma recalcification times and TAT levels were determined (Figure 1C). Compared with saline, 50 mg/kg of polyP shortened the recalcification time in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 1C) up to 2.7-fold in wild-type mice (from  $ 898 \pm 58 $  to  $ 394 \pm 37 $  s; P < 0.001) and up to 2.5-fold in  $ HRG^{-/-} $  mice (from  $ 716 \pm 78 $  to  $ 289 \pm 29 $  s; P < 0.0001). PolyP increased TAT levels in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 1D), and the levels were significantly higher in  $ HRG^{-/-} $  mice than in wild-type mice (P < 0.001). Compared with saline, polyP increased the TAT levels up to 10-fold in wild-type mice (from  $ 4 \pm 0.6 $  to  $ 50 \pm 6 $  ng/mL; P < 0.0001) and up to 14-fold in  $ HRG^{-/-} $  mice (from  $ 8 \pm 1 $  to  $ 71 \pm 8 $  ng/mL; P < 0.0001). Likewise, polyP induced dose-dependent fibrin deposition in the lungs, which was quantified by immunofluorescence using a fibrin-directed antibody (Figure 1E and 1F). Fibrin deposition was greater in  $ HRG^{-/-} $  mice than in wild-type mice (P = 0.005). For example, compared with saline-treated mice, treatment with 50 mg/kg of polyP enhanced fibrin deposition by  $ 10 \pm 4\% $  (P < 0.0001) in wild-type mice and by  $ 18 \pm 4\% $  (P < 0.0001) in  $ HRG^{-/-} $  mice, a statistically significant difference (P = 0.005). Therefore, the procoagulant and prothrombotic effects of polyP are greater in  $ HRG^{-/-} $  mice than in wild-type mice.

To determine whether thrombosis is responsible for the toxicity of polyP, mice were injected with 10 mg/kg of enoxaparin or saline 30 minutes before the administration of 50 mg/kg of polyP. Alternatively, 50 mg/kg of polyP was given to mice that were pretreated for 4 weeks with an FXII-directed or a control ASO (Figure S3). Although FXII depletion and enoxaparin significantly prolonged the recalcification clotting times (P<0.001), reduced the TAT levels (P<0.001; Figure 2C and 2D), and attenuated fibrin deposition in the lungs (P<0.0001; Figure 2E and 2F), neither intervention improved survival (Figure 2A and 2B). These findings suggest that overwhelming thrombosis is not the major driver of the lethal effects of polyP. This concept was supported by our failure to detect thrombosis in the coronary, cerebral, or main pulmonary arteries, which could cause death in minutes.

## I ntravenous polyP Induces ECG Changes

To assess the potential cardiotoxicity of polyP, the ECG was continuously monitored. Neither anesthesia nor surgery was associated with changes in the ECG (Figure 3A and 3B). However, injection of synthetic or platelet-derived polyP induced dose-dependent ECG changes, including conduction defects (premature ventricular contractions and first-, second-, or third-degree atrioventricular block) and ST-segment elevation or depression (Figure 3C through 3H). The ECG abnormalities were more marked in  $ HRG^{-/-} $ mice than in wild-type mice (Figure 3I through 3K). For example, 10 mg/kg of polyP induced first-degree block in wild-type mice and second-degree block in  $ HRG^{-/-} $ mice (Figure 3I and 3J). With 30 mg/kg polyP, wild-type mice exhibited first- or second-degree block, whereas in addition to the third-degree block, ST-segment elevation or depression was observed in  $ HRG^{-/-} $ mice. Neither enoxaparin nor FXII knockdown reversed the ECG changes associated with polyP injection. These observations suggest that polyP has toxic effects on the heart that are unrelated to thrombosis.

## PolyP Increases the Levels of Cardiac and Kidney Biomarkers

To further examine the potential cardiotoxicity and kidney toxicity of polyP, serum levels of troponin I and CK-MB were measured as were the creatinine levels. Compared with saline, polyP increased the levels of troponin I and CK-MB in a dose-dependent manner. Troponin I levels were increased by 2.4-fold  $ (P=0.003) $  and 3.2-fold  $ (P<0.001) $  in mice treated with 30 and 50 mg/kg polyP, respectively (Figure 4A). The levels of troponin I were significantly higher in  $ HRG^{-/-} $ mice than in wild-type mice (Figure 4A and 4B) both with the 30-mg/kg  $ (P=0.01) $  and 50-mg/kg polyP dose  $ (P<0.001) $ .

## PolyP Is Toxic for Cultured Cells and HRG Is Protective

To evaluate the cellular toxicity of polyP and the protective effects of HRG, a cultured murine cardiomyocyte cell line (HL-1) and cultured HEK293 cells were exposed to polyP in the absence or presence of HRG. The doses of polyP were chosen to be comparable with the levels achieved in mice given intravenous polyP. Exposure of HL-1 cells to polyP resulted in a time- and dose-dependent increase in the levels of LDH in the culture medium (Figure 6A; Figure S9). The increase in LDH levels likely reflects cell damage because there was an associated increase in F-actin staining (Figure 6B). Furthermore, 7 mmol/L polyP, a concentration similar to that achieved in mice given a 50-mg/kg dose of polyP, disrupted the cell cytoskeleton in a time-dependent manner and caused blebbing of the cell membrane, cell shrinkage, and nuclear condensation. In cells exposed to polyP, HRG significantly (P=0.009) decreased LDH levels in a concentration-dependent manner and reduced F-actin staining (Figure 6C and 6D). PolyP had similar effects on HEK293 cells (Figure S9). Therefore, polyP is toxic for both HL-1 and HEK293 cells and HRG protects against its toxic effects.

## DISCUSSION

Prior studies have shown that when administered to mice intravenously in high doses, both short-chain and long-chain polyP cause death within minutes, which has been attributed to overwhelming thrombosis. $ ^{[7,28,29,34]} $  We have previously shown that HRG protects mice from thrombosis induced by intraperitoneal injection of short-chain polyP by binding polyP and neutralizing its activity or binding FXIIa and attenuating its procoagulant activity. $ ^{[25]} $  The current study was undertaken to determine whether HRG and FXII have the same opposing effects with polyP in a murine model wherein polyp is given intravenously in high doses. We show that (1) at high doses, intravenous short-chain polyP is lethal in mice; (2) death is not due to thrombosis because neither enoxaparin nor FXII depletion enhances survival; (3) death likely reflects cardiotoxicity because polyP induces ECG abnormalities, increases the levels of troponin I and CK-MB in a dose-dependent manner, and is toxic for cultured HL-1 cells; and (4) HRG protects against the thrombotic and cardiotoxic effects of polyP. Therefore, HRG modulates both the prothrombotic effects of polyP and its toxic effects on cells.

Previous studies have shown that intravenous polyP is lethal when given to mice in doses up to 300 mg/kg, $ ^{[7,28,29,34]} $  In our study, the lethal dose of polyP in wild-type mice was  $ \geq $ 50 mg/kg, which corresponds to blood levels of at least 1 mg/mL. This amount is equivalent to a phosphate monomer concentration of about 7 mmol/L. To put this into perspective, the concentration of polyP released from activated platelets in whole blood is estimated to reach 2 to 7  $ \mu $ mol/L and possibly higher in the vicinity of platelet-rich thrombi. $ ^{[6]} $  Although the doses of polyP used in our study are higher than the physiological levels of polyP, we used these high doses to investigate whether HRG modulates the toxic effects of polyP and to determine whether mortality is due to thrombosis, as per the current dogma, or due to cardiotoxicity.

FXII knockdown or enoxaparin reduced TAT levels and attenuated fibrin deposition in the lungs indicating protection against the thrombotic effects of polyP. However, neither of the anticoagulant strategies protected against the cardiotoxic effects of polyP, nor did they prevent the increase in creatinine levels. These findings suggest that the toxic effect of polyP on cells is distinct from its prothrombotic effects. That FXII knockdown failed to protect mice from the lethal effects of polyP is at odds with a previous report that FXII $ ^{-/-} $  mice are protected against the lethal effect of high-dose intravenous polyP. These divergent findings may reflect incomplete FXII depletion with the dose of the FXII-directed ASO used in our study or a unique feature of the FXII $ ^{-/-} $  strain of mice used in the previous study. Regardless of the divergent effects of high-dose polyP on mortality with FXII deficiency and knockdown, we show that, like FXII knockdown, enoxaparin attenuates the prothrombotic effects of polyP but does not affect mortality. Therefore, our findings suggest that the lethal effect of polyP is not the result of overwhelming thrombosis.

Supplementation with HRG rescued HRG $ ^{-/-} $  mice from the prothrombotic and cardiotoxic effects of 30 mg/kg of polyP, suggesting that HRG modulates the cardiotoxicity of polyP via a mechanism distinct from its capacity to bind FXIIa and attenuate its procoagulant activity. Thus, it is likely that HRG neutralizes polyP in vivo. This concept is supported by our previous observation that with intraperitoneal injection of polyP, the plasma polyP concentration was 2-fold higher in HRG $ ^{-/-} $  mice than in wild-type mice. $ ^{[25]} $  Previously, we showed that HRG binds polyP and attenuates its capacity to activate FXII or to promote FXIIa-mediated FXI activation. $ ^{[25,26]} $  The results of the current study suggest that by binding polyP, HRG also attenuates its cardiotoxic effects in mice. Therefore, HRG modulates both the prothrombotic and cytotoxic effects of polyP.

The findings that polyP increases the levels of troponin, CK-MB, and creatinine and impair the viability of HL-1 cells, a murine cardiomyocyte cell line, and HEK293 cells, a human kidney cell line, in a dose-dependent manner suggest that the cytotoxic effects of polyP are not cell or organ specific. This observation is consistent with previous reports that polyP induces damage in neurocytes and various blood cells, as well as cardiomyocytes. $ ^{[35,36]} $  In cardiomyocytes, polyP activates the mitochondrial permeability transition pore allowing the influx of ions, including calcium, $ ^{[21,36,37]} $  PolyP binds calcium to form apatite, a calcium-phosphate complex. $ ^{[17,20,38-40]} $  Apatite adversely affects mitochondrial permeability transition pore homeostasis and increases the frequency of mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening. $ ^{[21,36,37]} $  By binding polyP, HRG has been shown to attenuate apatite formation, $ ^{[39,40]} $  which may explain why HRG protects cultured HL-1 cells from the toxic effects of polyP.

A potential limitation of this study is the use of wild-type mice as controls instead of  $ HRG^{+/+} $  littermates. Although differences between the wild-type C57BL/6J and the  $ HRG^{-/-}C57BL/6 $  N strains have been reported, $ ^{[41]} $  these are unlikely to have influenced our findings because (1) to the best of our knowledge, there are no reported differences in pathways related to thrombosis, (2) the studies lasted  $ \leq30 $  minutes, a time frame that is too short to

be influenced by strain-related differences, (3) the effect of polyP and saline was compared in both strains, and (4) supplemental HRG rendered the results in HRG $ ^{+/+} $  mice similar to those in wild-type mice. Therefore, the use of wild-type mice as controls instead of HRG $ ^{+/+} $  littermates is unlikely to be a drawback in this study.

In summary, the results of this study suggest that intravenous polyP has numerous physiological effects. At low doses, polyP causes thrombosis, whereas at higher doses, it has cytotoxic effects that lead to organ dysfunction. By binding polyP, HRG modulates both phenomena, thereby adding to the repertoire by which HRG downregulates coagulation.
