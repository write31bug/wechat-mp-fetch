# Identification of the histidine-rich glycoprotein domains responsible for contact pathway inhibition

## Abstract

Background: Previously, we showed that histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) binds factor (F) XIIa with high affinity, inhibits FXII autoactivation and FXIIa-mediated activation of FXI, and attenuates ferric chloride-induced arterial thrombosis in mice. Therefore, HRG downregulates the contact pathway in vitro and in vivo.

Objective: To identify the domains on HRG responsible for contact pathway inhibition.

Methods: Recombinant HRG domain constructs (N-terminal [N1, N2, and N1N2], proline-rich regions, histidine-rich region [HRR], and C-terminal) were expressed and purified. The affinities of plasma-derived HRG, HRG domain constructs, and synthetic HRR peptides for FXII, FXIIa,  $ \beta $ -FXIIa, and polyphosphate (polyP) were determined using surface plasmon resonance, and their effects on polyP-induced FXII autoactivation, FXIIa-mediated activation of FXI and prekallikrein, the activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and thrombin generation were examined.

Results: HRG and HRG domain constructs bind FXIIa, but not FXII or  $ \beta $ -FXII. HRR, N1, and N1N2 bind FXIIa with affinities comparable with that of HRG, whereas the remaining domains bind with lower affinity. Synthetic HRR peptides bind FXIIa and polyP with high affinity. HRG and HRR significantly inhibit FXII autoactivation and prolong the APTT. Like HRG, synthetic HRR peptides inhibit FXII autoactivation, attenuate FXIIa-mediated activation of prekallikrein and FXI, prolong the APTT, and attenuate thrombin generation.

Conclusion: The interaction of HRG with FXIIa and polyP is predominantly mediated by the HRR domain. Like intact HRG, HRR downregulates the contact pathway and contributes to HRG-mediated down regulation of coagulation.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The contact pathway is triggered when factor (F) XII binds to negatively charged surfaces and undergoes autoactivation. $ ^{1,2} $  Such surfaces include nucleic acids released from cells undergoing apoptosis, autophagy, or necrosis, neutrophil extracellular traps extruded from activated neutrophils, and short chain polyphosphate (polyP) consisting of 70–100 phosphate units released from activated platelets. $ ^{3-7} $ 

Binding of FXII to negatively charged surfaces induces its autoactivation via a conformational change that is enhanced in the presence of  $ Zn^{2+} $ . $ ^{8,9} $  FXII autoactivation occurs by cleavage of the Arg353-Val354 bond, which converts single-chain FXII into its activated, two-chain form. $ ^{10-12} $   $ \alpha $ -FXIIa (FXIIa) propagates coagulation by activating FXI, which circulates in complex with high molecular weight kininogen (HK), and culminates in thrombin generation and fibrin formation. $ ^{13,14} $  Recent studies suggest that FXII, which is dispensable for hemostasis, is important for thrombus growth. $ ^{15-23} $  Therefore, FXIIa inhibition may attenuate thrombosis without disrupting hemostasis.

Previously, we showed that histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) binds FXIIa with high affinity, thereby inhibiting FXII autoactivation and FXIIa-mediated activation of FXI, $ ^{24} $  and attenuating ferric chloride ( $ FeCl_{3} $ )-induced arterial thrombosis in mice. $ ^{25} $  HRG is a 75-kDa glycoprotein that circulates in plasma at a concentration of  $ \sim $ 1–3  $ \mu $ M and is implicated in diverse processes including coagulation, fibrinolysis, immunity, and angiogenesis. $ ^{24,26-28} $  Human HRG consists of two cystatin-like N-terminal domains (N1 and N2), a central histidine-rich region (HRR) flanked by two proline-rich regions (PRR1 and PPR2), and a C-terminal domain (COOH). $ ^{29,30} $  The HRR is unique because of its unusually high content of histidine (13% of total amino acids) and the presence of 12 tandem repeats of a Gly-His-His-Pro-His consensus sequence. $ ^{29} $  The multidomain structure of HRG endows it with the capacity to bind many ligands including fibrinogen, plasminogen, thrombospondin, and heparin. $ ^{26,27,31,32} $  The HRR domain of HRG binds divalent metal cations (e.g.,  $ Zn^{2+} $ ,  $ Ni^{2+} $ ,  $ Cu^{2+} $ ,  $ Co^{2+} $ ) and undergoes a conformational change, which may influence its biological activity. $ ^{26,27,33} $  HRG also binds nucleic acids and polyP and attenuates their capacity to activate the contact pathway. $ ^{34,35} $  Therefore, HRG is a multifunctional protein.

HRG downregulates coagulation in at least two ways. First, HRG binds to an exosite on FXIIa and prevents its interactions with FXII and FXI. $ ^{24} $  HRG does not bind to zymogen FXII, FXI, FXIa, or FIX. $ ^{24} $  Second, HRG binds polyP and other polyanions and attenuates their capacity to activate FXII. $ ^{24,34,35} $  Although HRG is a potent regulator of the intrinsic pathway of coagulation in vitro $ ^{24} $  and in vivo, $ ^{25} $  the HRG domains responsible for its interaction with FXIIa and polyanions such as polyP remain elusive. To identify these domains, we generated recombinant constructs of individual HRG domains and synthetic HRR peptide analogs and determined their (1) affinities for FXIIa, FXII, β-FXIIa, and polyP; (2) inhibitory effects on polyP-induced FXII autoactivation, FXIIa-mediated activation of FXI, and prekallikrein (PK); and (3) effects on the activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) and polyP-induced thrombin generation.

## ESSENTIALS

• Histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) downregulates the contact pathway.

- The histidine-rich region (HRR) domain of HRG modulates factor XIIa activity.

• HRR analogs recapitulate the effects of intact HRG.

- Like HRG, HRR analogs may attenuate thrombosis without disrupting hemostasis.

## 2 | METHODS

### 2.1 | Reagents

Oligonucleotide primers were from MOBIX (McMaster University, Hamilton, ON) and are described in Table S1. HRG (NM_000412) Human Tagged ORF Clone (cDNA) was purchased from OriGene Global (Rockville, MD). The expression vector, pMAL-c5e, and BL21-Gold (DE3) Escherichia coli were from New England Biolabs Ltd. (Whitby, ON). Peptide analogs of the HRR containing one or four repeats of the His-His-Pro-His-Gly (HHPHG) $ _{1-4} $  consensus sequence, $ ^{36} $  a scrambled peptide containing five repeats of HAHG, Scr(HAHG) $ _{5} $ H, and poly-His peptides consisting of six or 12 His residues (H-6 and H-12) were from Biomatik (Cambridge, ON) (Table S2). Short chain polyP consisting of 70–100 phosphate units (P70.5) was a generous gift from Dr. Thomas Stoffel (BK Giulini GmbH, Ladenburg, Germany). Biotinylated Phe-Pro-Arg-chloromethyl ketone was from Hematologic Technologies Inc. (Essex Junction, VT). Zinc chloride (ZnCl $ _{2} $ ), silicon dioxide (silica; 5–15 nm), IPTG, and EDTA were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Human FXII,  $ \alpha $ -FXIIa,  $ \beta $ -FXIIa, FXI, PK, HK, and corn trypsin inhibitor (CTI) were from Enzyme Research Laboratories (South Bend, IN). Unless otherwise specified, FXIIa will refer to  $ \alpha $ -FXIIa. RecombiPlasTin 2G and APTT-SP (liquid) were purchased from Instrumentation Laboratory Company (Bedford, MA). Phosphatidylcholine-phosphatidylserine (3:1) vesicles were prepared as previously described. $ ^{37} $  A sheep polyclonal antibody against human HRG and a polyclonal goat anti-human FXII antibody were from Affinity Biologicals (Ancaster, ON). The HRG-directed antibody was affinity purified as previously described. $ ^{25} $  Horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate was prepared using a Lightning-Link HRP conjugation kit from Novus Biologicals (Centennial, CO) according to the manufacturer's protocol. A polyclonal rabbit antibody against maltose-binding protein (MBP) antibody was from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Burlington, ON). BIAcore sensor chips (CM4 and CM5) and amine coupling kit were from Cytiva (Mississauga, ON). HRG was isolated from human plasma using a Ni-NTA agarose column and human plasma was depleted of HRG by affinity chromatography as previously described. $ ^{24} $

### 2.2 | Cloning, expression, and purification of MBP-HRG domain constructs

#### 2.2.1 | Cloning

Primers were designed to polymerase chain reaction-amplify individual and adjacent domains of human HRG (Table S1). Polymerase chain reaction products were gel purified, digested with Ava1 or Nde1 and BamH1 (New England Biolabs Ltd.), and then ligated into a pMAL-c5e expression vector containing a  $ NH_{2} $ -terminal MBP-tag. The domain constructs were as follows: MBP-N1, MBP-N2, MBP-N1N2, MBP-PRR1, MBP-HRR, MBP-PRR2, and MBP-COOH (Figure S1). All clones were verified by Sanger sequencing.

#### 2.2.2 | Expression

MBP-HRG domain plasmids were transformed into BL21 (DE3) E coli cells and allowed to grow for 1–2 h at  $ 37^{\circ} $ C until reaching mid-log phase ( $ A_{600} = \sim0.6 $ ). Expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG for an additional 4 h at  $ 37^{\circ} $ C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000g for 20 min and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 25 ml of column buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM sodium azide, pH 7.4) in the presence of Complete Protease Inhibitor (Sigma). Bacteria were placed in an ice water bath and subjected to lysis by sonication in 5 s on/off pulses for 20 min using a FB120 Sonic Dismembrator fitted with a CL-18 probe (Fisher Scientific). Lysed bacteria were subjected to centrifugation at 20 000g for 20 min. The supernatant was harvested and diluted 1:6 with column buffer and subjected to amylose affinity chromatography at  $ 4^{\circ} $ C.

#### 2.2.3 | Purification

Amylose resin (New England Biolabs) in a 10-ml column was washed with five volumes of column buffer. Diluted supernatant was loaded onto the column and the flow through was collected. The column was washed with 12 volumes of column buffer and protein was eluted with 15 ml of 10 mM maltose (New England Biolabs) in column buffer. One-milliliter fractions were collected, and protein content was assessed by measuring absorbance at 280 nm and 320 nm. Protein-containing fractions were pooled; subjected to buffer exchange four times into 20 mM HEPES-OH, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4 (HBS); concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Units (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.); and stored in aliquots at  $ -80^{\circ} $ C. Protein concentrations were determined by measuring absorbance and using calculated extinction coefficients (Expasy ProtParam online tool).

### 2.3 | SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis

Purified recombinant MBP-HRG domain constructs were subjected to SDS-PAGE analysis on 4%-15% polyacrylamide gels (Bio-Rad Laboratories Ltd.) in the presence of 0.1% SDS and 0.14 M β-mercaptoethanol. Separated proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad) overnight at 4°C using a Mini-PROTEAN Tetra electrophoresis system (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 1% BSA in 50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4 containing 0.005% Tween20 and probed with affinity-purified HRP-conjugated polyclonal sheep anti-HRG antibody (0.2 μg/ml) for 2 h or affinity-purified polyclonal rabbit antibody against MBP (0.1 μg/ml) overnight at 4°C. Blots probed with the antibody against MBP were then incubated with 0.2 μg/ml HRP-linked secondary anti-rabbit antibody (Cedarlane) for 2 h at 25°C. Bound HRP was detected with the Immune-Star HRP kit (Bio-Rad) and chemiluminescence was quantified using Chemi-Doc (Bio-Rad).

### 2.4 | Surface plasmon resonance

The affinities of MBP-HRG constructs and synthetic HRR peptides for FXIIa, β-FXIIa, and FXII, and polyP were quantified using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) on a BIAcore T200. Streptavidin (ThermoFisher) was attached onto flow cells of a CM4 sensor chip to 2000–3000 response units (RU) using an amine coupling kit. $ ^{24} $  FXIIa or β-FXIIa, labeled at the active site with biotinylated Phe-Pro-Arg-chloromethyl ketone, was absorbed to immobilized streptavidin to  $ \sim $ 500 RU at a flow rate of 10  $ \mu $ l/min at 25°C. FXII was immobilized directly onto a flow cell of a CM4 chip to  $ \sim $ 500 RU by amine coupling. Unmodified polyP was immobilized on a CM5 chip derivatized with a diamine linker. Ethylenediamine first was attached to the flow cell using an amine coupling protocol (Cytiva), where ethylenediamine was substituted for ethanolamine. PolyP was immediately attached to the free amine with modifications to the method of Choi et al $ ^{38} $  and Thermo Scientific Tech Tip. $ ^{39} $  PolyP was dissolved in water to 500 mg/ml at 37°C. A solution consisting of 230  $ \mu $ l of polyP, 50  $ \mu $ l of 1 M EDC (in 100 mM 3-(N-morpholino)-propane sulfonic acid [MOPS], pH 6.5), 25  $ \mu $ l of 0.1 M imidazole, pH 6.0, and 192  $ \mu $ l of 100 mM MOPS, pH 6.5 was mixed and supplemented with an additional 25  $ \mu $ l of 0.1 M imidazole. The solution was immediately injected into the flow cell at 5  $ \mu $ l/min for 50 min, followed by two additional injections for 15 min each. This resulted in an increase in RU of 140. The running buffer was 100 mM MOPS, pH 6.5. The control flow cell was blocked with ethanolamine using amine coupling.

Binding experiments were performed at  $ 25^{\circ} $ C in HBS buffer containing 0.005% Tween-20 and  $ 13 \mu $ M  $ ZnCl_{2} $  at a flow rate of  $ 50 \mu $ l/min. Increasing concentrations of analytes, 0–500 nM HRG, 0–5000 nM HRG domain constructs, or 0–8000 nM synthetic HRR peptides, were injected into both flow cells for 300 s, followed by running buffer alone for 900 s. Flow cells were regenerated with 250 mM NaCl, 62.5 mM imidazole, and 5 mM EDTA for 60 s. Reciprocal experiments were performed by SPR to determine the affinity of FXII and FXIIa for HRG in the presence of  $ 13 \mu $ M  $ ZnCl_{2} $ . Biotinylated HRG was absorbed to immobilized streptavidin onto a CM5 chip to 560 RU at a flow rate of  $ 10 \mu $ l/min at  $ 25^{\circ} $ C. $ ^{24} $  Binding experiments were performed as previously described with increasing

concentrations of 0–1000 nM FXII and FXIIa. Sensorgrams were analyzed with BIAcore T200 evaluation software Version 1.0 (BIAcore). Dissociation constant ( $ K_{d} $ ) values were determined from equilibrium values or calculated from rate constants using 1:1 Langmuir analysis by the instrument software.

### 2.5 | FXII autoactivation

In a 100- $ \mu $ l reaction volume, silica (40  $ \mu $ g/ml) or polyP (8  $ \mu $ g/mL) was added to wells of a 96-well plate containing 6  $ \mu $ M  $ ZnCl_{2} $  and 100 nM FXII in HBS-Tw20 in the absence or presence of MBP-HRG constructs or synthetic HRR peptides at concentrations up to 5000 nM. After 15 min incubation, 200  $ \mu $ M S-2302 was added to each well and absorbance was monitored at 405 nm every 10 s for 30 min at 37°C using a Thermomax microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). FXIIa generation was quantified by dividing rates of absorbance change by the specific activity of 100 nM FXIIa, which was determined separately. Plots of the concentrations of FXIIa generated versus the concentrations of HRG or HRG derivatives were subjected to nonlinear regression analysis using TableCurve 2D Version 4 (Jandel Scientific Software, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and fitted to a rectangular hyperbola so that the concentration required for 50% inhibition of FXII activation ( $ IC_{50} $ ) could be calculated.

### 2.6 | FXI activation by FXIIa

In 100- $ \mu $ l reaction volumes, HRG or synthetic HRR peptides (0–5000 nM) were added to wells containing 6  $ \mu $ M  $ ZnCl_{2} $ , 2 nM FXIIa, 100 nM FXI, and 0.5  $ \mu $ g/ml polyP in HBS-Tw20. After a 5-min incubation at 37°C, 1  $ \mu $ M CTI was added to inhibit FXIIa. After an additional 5-min incubation, 200  $ \mu $ M S-2366 was added and absorbance was monitored at 405 nm as described. FXIa generated was quantified by comparison with a standard curve of FXIa activity. The percentages of FXI activation were normalized relative to that obtained in the absence of HRG or synthetic HRR peptides and plotted against analyte concentration.  $ IC_{50} $  values were determined as described previously.

### 2.7 | Prekallikrein activation by FXIIa

In 100- $ \mu $ l reactions, HRG or synthetic HRR peptides (0–5000 nM) were added to wells of a 96-well plate containing 120 nM prekalikrein, 6  $ \mu $ M  $ ZnCl_{2} $ , 5  $ \mu $ g/ml polyP, and 2 mM  $ CaCl_{2} $  in 0.005% Tween20. To initiate the reactions, 2 nM FXIIa was added, and the mixtures were incubated for 10 min at 37°C before addition of 1  $ \mu $ M CTI to inhibit FXIIa. After a 5-min incubation at the same temperature, 200  $ \mu $ M S-2302 and 3 mM EDTA were added, and absorbance was measured at 405 nm for 60 min. Kallikrein generation was quantified by comparison with a standard curve of kallikrein activity.  $ IC_{50} $  values were determined as described previously.

### 2.8 | Plasma clotting assays

In a 96-well plate, 90- $ \mu $ l aliquots of control or HRG-depleted human plasma containing 0–5  $ \mu $ M HRG or HRR peptides or 0–8  $ \mu $ M MBP-HRG domain constructs were added to wells containing either 10  $ \mu $ l of a 1:10 dilution of aPTT-SP or 10  $ \mu $ l of a 1:100 dilution of RecombiPlasTin 2G reagent. After a 10-min incubation at 37°C, clotting was initiated by addition of 10  $ \mu $ l of 260 mM CaCl $ _{2} $  (final concentration 26 mM). Absorbance was monitored at 405 nm for 1 h at 37°C and the time to reach half-maximal absorbance as determined by instrument software was used as the clot time.

### 2.9 | Thrombin generation assay

Thrombin generation was quantified as previously described with some modifications. $ ^{[25]} $  HRG-depleted human plasma (50  $ \mu $ l) containing 0–20  $ \mu $ M HRG or HRR peptides was added to the wells of a black 96-well plate (Costar, Lowell, MA). To the wells was added 40  $ \mu $ g/ml polyP and 15  $ \mu $ M phosphatidylcholine-phosphatidylserine. After a 10-min incubation at 37°C, 15 mM CaCl $ _{2} $  was added and thrombin generation was monitored by measuring the hydrolysis of 1 mM Z-Gly-Gly-Arg AMC (Bachem, Bubendorf, Switzerland) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 360 nm and 460 nm, respectively, for 90 min at 37°C using a SpectraMax M5e plate reader (Molecular Devices). Thrombin generation profiles and data were analyzed using Technothrombin TGA software (Technoclone, Vienna, Austria), as previously described. $ ^{[25]} $ 

### 2.10 | Statistical analysis

Results are presented as the mean ± SD. Significance of differences was determined using one or two-way analysis of variance followed by the Holm-Sidak comparison test. SigmaPlot (Systat Software, San Jose, CA) and IBM SPSS Statistics software (Armonk, NY) were used to create graphs and perform the statistical analyses, respectively. For all analyses, p values < .05 were considered statistically significant.

## 3 | RESULTS

### 3.1 | HRR and N1 domains of HRG bind FXIIa and polyP with high affinity

To identify the domains of HRG responsible for its interaction with FXIIa, individual domains were subcloned and expressed in E coli (Figure 1A). Recombinant MBP-HRG domain constructs (N1, N2, and N1N2; PRR1, PRR2; HRR; and COOH) were purified from E coli lysates using amylose affinity chromatography. SDS-PAGE (Figure 1B) and Western blot analysis (Figure 1C) revealed the expected insert sizes of the constructs.

The affinities of the MBP-HRG domain constructs for FXIIa, β-FXIIa, FXII, and polyP were determined by SPR and compared with the affinity of plasma-derived HRG. In the presence of  $ Zn^{2+} $ , MBP-HRG domain constructs bind to immobilized FXIIa with varying affinities (Table 1), but do not bind to immobilized FXII or β-FXIIa. Like HRG, MBP-HRR binds to FXIIa with high affinity ( $ K_{d} $  values of  $ 22 \pm 4 $  and  $ 123 \pm 7 $  nM, respectively). MBP-N1 and MBP-N1N2 also have similar affinities ( $ K_{d} $  values of  $ 92 \pm 6 $  and  $ 155 \pm 26 $  nM, respectively). MBP-PRR2 binds to FXIIa with moderate affinity ( $ K_{d} $  value of  $ 307 \pm 110 $  nM). In contrast, the remaining domains (MBP-N2, MBP-PRR1, MBP-COOH) bind FXIIa with significantly lower affinities than HRG. To exclude the possibility that the method of FXII or FXIIa immobilization could influence the binding data, reciprocal binding experiments were performed with immobilized HRG. FXIIa binds HRG with a  $ K_{d} $  value of  $ 67 \pm 0.9 $  nM (Figure S2), whereas FXII does not bind HRG (not shown).

Next, we determined the affinities of HRG and HRG domain constructs for polyP. Like HRG, MBP-HRR binds polyP with high affinity ( $ K_{d} $  values of  $ 115 \pm 19 $  and  $ 187 \pm 19 $  nM, respectively) (Table 1). MBP-N1, MBP-N2, and MBP-N1N2 also bind polyP ( $ K_{d} $  values of  $ 71 \pm 1 $ ,  $ 140 \pm 1 $ , and  $ 243 \pm 1 $ , respectively). MBP-PRR1 and MBP-PRR2 bind polyP with lower affinity ( $ K_{d} $  values of  $ 949 \pm 112 $  and  $ 3400 \pm 10 $  nM, respectively), whereas MBP control and MBP-COOH do not bind. Together, these findings suggest that, although the interaction of HRG with FXIIa and polyP may involve multiple domains, the HRR and N1 domains exhibit the highest affinity.
