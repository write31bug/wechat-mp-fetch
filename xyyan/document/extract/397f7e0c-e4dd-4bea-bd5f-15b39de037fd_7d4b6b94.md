# Enhanced blood coagulation and fibrinolysis in mice lacking histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG)

Summary. Histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) is a serum protein belonging to the cystatin superfamily. HRG may play a regulatory role in hemostasis and innate immunity. However, this role is uncertain because of a lack of rigorous testing in an animal model. We generated mice lacking the translation start point of exon 1 of the Hrg gene, effectively resulting in a null mutation ( $ Hrg^{-/-} $ ). The mice were viable and fertile but had no HRG in their blood. Antithrombin activity in the plasma of  $ Hrg^{-/-} $  mice was higher than in the plasma of heterozygous  $ Hrg^{+/-} $  or wild-type  $ Hrg^{+/+} $  mice. The prothrombin time was shorter in  $ Hrg^{-/-} $  mice than in  $ Hrg^{+/-} $  and  $ Hrg^{+/+} $  mice. Bleeding time after tail tip amputation in  $ Hrg^{-/-} $  mice was shorter than in  $ Hrg^{+/+} $  mice. The spontaneous fibrinolytic activity in clotted blood of  $ Hrg^{-/-} $  mice was higher than in  $ Hrg^{+/+} $  mice. These findings suggest that HRG plays a role as both an anticoagulant and an antifibrinolytic modifier, and may regulate platelet function in vivo.

Keywords: blood proteins, glycoproteins, hemostasis, HRG, knockout mice.

## I ntroduction

Human histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) is a single-chain glycoprotein with a molecular weight of 67 000; it is composed of 507 amino acid residues. HRG belongs to the cystatin superfamily of proteins like fetuins and kininogens  $ [1-3] $ . The genes for fetuin-A, fetuin-B, HRG and kininogen cluster head to tail on chromosome 3 in the human genome and on chromosome 16 in mouse. The Hrg gene includes seven exons and six introns. The N-terminal region of the protein consists of two cystatin-like domains D1 and D2 which are each encoded by three exons, exons I to III and exons IV to VI, respectively. Exon VII encodes for a large C-terminal domain, whose N-terminal region is very rich in proline and histidine residues. HRG is exclusively synthesized in the liver, but it becomes associated with platelets and megakaryocytes and can be released after thrombin stimulation  $ [4] $ . Mature HRG circulates in the blood at a relatively high concentration of up to 150 mg L $ ^{-1} $  ( $ \sim $ 2  $ \mu $ mol L $ ^{-1} $ ).

HRG binds to heparin [5,6], plasminogen [7,8] and fibrinogen [9] suggesting a role in hemostasis. Several individuals with a congenital deficiency of HRG were discovered in three families with thrombophilia  $ [10–12] $  corroborating such a role. Molecular binding of HRG to multiple ligands is mediated through specific binding domains, which bind independent of each other [13]. A low-affinity binding site for the cell surface glycosaminoglycan heparan sulfate was recently mapped to the N-terminal tandem cystatin domains (N1N2) [14]. Cell binding of HRG was enhanced by  $ Zn^{2+} $  in an indirect fashion by binding to the histidine-proline-rich domain. This binding behavior can greatly influence HRG bioactivity at sites of local changes in pH value and  $ Zn^{2+} $  concentration [15], suggesting that HRG may be a tunable adaptor protein for several ligands involved in inflammation and tissue remodelling. Such a mechanism was indeed experimentally verified for HRG binding of plasminogen [16]. The authors proposed that under conditions of low pH and elevated free  $ Zn^{2+} $ , HRG acts as a high-affinity receptor for plasminogen thus enhancing the migratory potential of cells.

In addition, HRG binds and transports divalent metals  $ [17,18] $ , to complement components and IgG, and inhibits T-cell proliferation  $ [19–21] $ . Recently, it was reported that HRG has potent antiangiogenic activity  $ [22,23] $ . Despite these abundant biological properties reported for HRG, the physiological

function of HRG has not been firmly established. To explore the physiological function of HRG, we cloned the mouse HRG gene and produced, by homologous recombination, mice lacking HRG.

## Materials and methods

All animal experiments in this study were performed complying with the German Animal Protection Law (Tierschutzgesetz) following procedures reviewed and approved by institutional and state animal protection boards.

## Targeting vector and homologous recombination

To construct a targeting vector for the Hrg gene, we first screened a mouse 129/SvJ genomic library with a 1.1-kilobase (kb) EcoRI fragment of rat HRG cDNA. The identity of the gene was confirmed by restriction mapping with rat and mouse Hrg cDNAs and by sequencing. A targeting vector was constructed following published protocols [24]. A 1.9-kb SalI-XbaI DNA fragment including a part of exon I of the mouse Hrg gene served as 5'-homologous region and a 7.8-kb BamHI genomic fragment of mouse Hrg, which contains exons II to VII served as a 3'-homologous region. Thus, the targeting vector replaced a 1.8-kb XbaI-BamHI fragment comprising a part of exon I including the translation start point with a neomycin cassette for positive clone selection (Fig. 1). Flanking TK cassettes for negative selection were also contained in the vector.

129/Ola embryonic stem cells (passage 10) were grown on a mouse embryonic fibroblast feeder layer and electroporated with 60  $ \mu $ g of PvuI-linearized replacement vector DNA following established protocols. ## Hematological analysis

Blood was collected by cardiac puncture and serum was obtained by centrifugation. Serum chemistry and hemograms were performed using a Vitros 250 analyzer (Orthochemical Diagnostics, Necher Giemlind, Germany) and a Celltek-M (Nihon Konden; Johnson & Johnson, Bad Hamburg, Germany) analyzer, respectively. For coagulation assays blood was anticoagulated with sodium citrate (9 : 1 v/v with 3.8% sodium citrate). Antithrombin activity was determined using the COAMATIC $ ^{\circledR} $  Antithrombin kit (Chromogenix, Orangeburg, NY, USA) and control plasma N (Dade Behring, Marburg, Germany).

Fibrinogen was determined using commercial kit 886 (Sigma, Taufkirchen, Germany). Prothrombin time was measured using a KC10A coagulometer (Amelung, Lemgo, Germany). Human HRG was purified from human plasma as described previously  $ [26] $ .

## Bleeding time

The tails of 2–4-month-old anesthetized mice (100 mg ketamine and 5 mg xylazine per kg intraperitoneally) were prewarmed for 5 min at 37 °C in a water bath, and a 5-mm segment of tail tip was amputated with a razor blade. The bleeding time was taken until the bleeding stopped.

## Whole blood clot lysis

Briefly, 100  $ \mu $ L of fresh blood collected by vena cava puncture was diluted 10-fold with saline containing  $ \sim $ 10 000 counts per

min human  $ {}^{125} $ I-labeled fibrinogen (Amersham, Buckinghamshire, UK) and was coagulated at  $ 37\ ^{\circ}C $  with  $ 8\ \muL $  of bovine thrombin ( $ 75\ U\ mL^{-1} $ ) (Sigma), and  $ 10\ \muL $  of  $ 0.75\ mol\ L^{-1}\ CaCl_{2} $  solution [27]. At the times indicated the  $ {}^{125} $ I content of  $ 50\ \muL $  aliquots of supernatant was measured using a gamma-counter (model LB 951 G; Berthold, Bad Wildbad, Germany). The release of  $ {}^{125} $ I-labeled fibrin degradation peptides was analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and autoradiography using  $ 10\ \muL $  of lyzed clot supernatant and NuPAGE $ ^{TM} $  acrylamide gels (Novex, Frankfurt, Germany).

## I nduction of skin wounds

Mice were anesthetized, shaved dorsolaterally and swabbed with 70% ethanol. A full thickness wound was inflicted using an 8-mm dermal biopsy punch. Wound areas were photographed daily and the wound area was measured histomorphometrically using National Institutes of Health IMAGE software.

## Results

## Structure of the Hrg gene locus

Figure 1 shows the structure of the mouse Hrg gene locus. The complete nucleotide sequence of 13 591 bases (AB055898) was determined by sequencing. The alignment with the cDNA sequence demonstrated that the gene for mouse Hrg was composed of six small exons I through VI (81–225 bp), and one large exon VII (905 bp). The size of introns varied from 677 bp to 2315 bp. This exon—intron structure was very similar to that of the human gene [28] where seven exons were likewise identified. The structural organization into two groups of three exons coding for a cystatin-like protein domain each, and one additional larger exon coding for a third protein domain specific for the respective protein is also present in the related fetuin family proteins, Fetuin-A/Ahsg and Fetuin-B/Fetub [24,29]. The genes encoding these proteins are neighbors on chromosome 16 in the mouse genome and therefore originated by gene duplication. A similar arrangement is conserved in the human genome.

## Hematological analysis

Routine hematology revealed similar blood cell counts for  $ Hrg^{-/-} $ mice and for wild-type mice, but monocytes were 2.5-fold more abundant in HRG-deficient than in wild-type mice ( $ 5.3 \pm 3.0\% $  vs.  $ 2.1 \pm 2.1\% $ , n = 8, P = 0.018). Serum activity GOT/AST, GPT/ALT, GGT, blood glucose, triglycerides, urea, creatinine, and electrolytes were all normal in HRG-deficient and wild-type mice suggesting that liver, heart and kidney metabolism were uncompromised by the lack of HRG (data not shown).

## Skin wound model

To evaluate if the observed differences in both coagulation and fibrinolysis would influence the healing of an external wound, which involves both thrombosis and fibrinolysis, we inflicted full thickness skin wounds to the back of  $ Hrg^{-/-} $ and wild-type mice. One day after excision of skin biopsies the wounds had shrunk to about 50% of the original wounded area in both wild-type and  $ Hrg^{-/-} $ mice. The wounds were typically covered by a dry eschar within 2 days (not shown). A detailed morphometric analysis of the wound healing was performed using electronic images of the wounds. Large variation of wound areas existed within experimental groups during the wound contraction period (day 1–4) regardless of their genotype. These differences leveled out during the first week. We did not observe statistically significant differences in the gross morphology (not shown) and timing of wound healing between wild-type and  $ Hrg^{-/-} $ mice indicating that the perturbation of plasmatic and platelet-mediated blood clotting observed ex vivo did not overtly influence the wound healing in this simple wounding model (data not shown).
