# HIGH LEVELS OF HISTIDINE-RICH GLYCOPROTEIN AND THROMBOTIC DIATHESIS

REPORT OF TWO UNRELATED FAMILIES

Giancarlo Castaman, Marco Ruggeri, Francesco Burei $ ^{*} $  and Francesco Rodeghiero

Department of Hematology and Hemophilia and Thrombosis Center, San Bortolo Hospital, Vicenza; *Department of Medical Emergency, Hospital of Treviso, Italy

(Received 1.9.1992; accepted in revised form 3.12.1992 by Editor S. Coccheri)

## Abstract

Two new families with history of thrombosis and high levels of histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) are described. The propositus of family 1 died of massive pulmonary embolism at age 34. Among his relatives, the mother and the maternal grandmother had suffered from deep and superficial vein thrombosis in their youth. A maternal aunt had several episodes of superficial vein thrombosis (SVT). High levels of HRG were found in the mother, three siblings and two nephews. In the second family, the proposita suffered from spontaneous deep vein thrombosis (DVT) at age 24. The paternal grandmother and a paternal aunt had several episodes of SVT and DVT. Also in this family, high levels of HRG cosegregated with thrombotic symptoms. These new families confirm that genetically transmitted high levels of HRG could be associated to familial and juvenile thrombophilia.

Histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) is a non-enzymatic plasma protein which interacts with the high affinity lysine-binding site of plasminogen (1) and binds heparin with high affinity (2). Through these interactions, HRG reduces the amount of free

plasminogen that binds to fibrin clot and lessens the amount of heparin available for the enhancement of thrombin-antithrombin III reaction. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that HRG is present in the  $ \alpha $ -granules of platelets from where it is released after thrombin stimulation (3). In this way, platelet-released HRG may achieve a high local concentration and play a significant role in modulating fibrinolysis in the microenvironment of the platelet plug.

Conceivably, high levels of this protein could be associated with thrombophilia and, in fact, recently a kindred with familial elevation of HRG and thrombotic symptoms in the propositus has been reported (4). Subsequently, additional cases have been published (5-8), sometime associated with high levels of plasminogen-activator inhibitor (PAI-1) (7,8).

In this study, we report two new families with autosomal dominant transmission of very high levels of HRG and evidence of thrombotic diathesis.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Coagulation assays

Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), thrombin time (TT) and fibrinogen were assayed on a laser-automated instrument (ACL, Paderno Dugnano, Italy), using Instrumental Laboratory (IL) reagents and according to the manufacturer's instructions. Antithrombin III (AT-III) activity was measured as heparin cofactor activity using the chromogenic substrate S-2238 (Kabi, Stockholm). Total and free protein S antigen was assayed by rocket immunoelectrophoresis (Asseraplate, Stago, Paris). Protein C activity was determined using the chromogenic substrate S-2366 (Kabi). Plasminogen and  $ \alpha $ -2-antiplasmin activities were measured using an amidolytic method with the chromogenic substrate S-2251 (Kabi). Heparin cofactor (HC) II activity was measured after activation with dermatan sulfate using the chromogenic substrate 2 ACOH.H-D-CHG-Gly-Arg-pNA (Channel Diagnostics, supplied by Cabru, Milan). PAI-1 activity was assayed using a chromogenic substrate (Kabi).

HRG antigen was measured by electroimmunoassay (Asseraplate, Stago). Crossed immunoelectrophoresis (CIE) of a mixture of normal plasma or serum HRG with that of subjects with high levels of HRG was carried out using 2.1 % of antiserum (Stago, Paris) in the second dimension. CIE was performed also with heparin (Liquemin, Roche, Basel) (16 U/mL, final concentration) in the first dimension.

## CASE REPORT

## 1 ) FAMILY B

The relatives (mother, an uncle, the 3 siblings, the 3 nephews and one niece) of a man died at age 34 of massive pulmonary

embolism after deep vein thrombosis (DVT) were investigated for coagulation defects predisposing to thrombophilia (Figure 1). The propositus (III. 4) at age 25 suffered from superficial vein thrombosis (SVT) and at age 34 was admitted to the hospital for spontaneous DVT. Intravenous heparin (25,000 units/day by continuous infusion) was started. After few hours, severe dyspnoea developed and a clinical diagnosis of pulmonary embolism was made. Heparin was augmented (37,500 U/day) and recombinant tissue-type plasminogen activator (100 mg over two hours) was started. Despite this treatment, the patient died for cardiac arrest. Autopsy revealed DVT of right leg and massive pulmonary embolism with upper right lobe infarction. In addition, signs of recent myocardial infarction were documented. Unfortunately, no plasma samples were available for coagulation studies.

The mother (II.3, aged 68) suffered from DVT at age 29 during puerperium after her third pregnancy and from repeated SVT episodes. The maternal grandmother (I.2) had DVT after delivery at age 32 and 35 as well as several episodes of SVT. A maternal aunt (II.2, aged 69) had several episodes of SVT. The maternal uncle (II.1, aged 71), the two sisters (III.2, aged 38, and III.3, aged 36), the brother (III.1, aged 40) and 3 nephews (IV.1, aged 4; IV.3, aged 14, and IV.4, aged 12) and the niece (IV.2, aged 6) were all asymptomatic.

## 2 ) FAMILY M

The proposita (III.1, Figure 2) was referred for spontaneous DVT confirmed by impedance pletysmography at age 24. She had had no surgical intervention or pregnancy. A paternal aunt (II.3, aged 56) had suffered from DVT after leg fracture, confirmed by venography, and the grandmother (I.2) from DVT after delivery and from repeated SVT. The two sisters, aged 23 and 21, and the father, aged 50, were asymptomatic.

## RESULTS

Table I summarizes the results of coagulation and fibrinolytic assays of the family members tested. PTT, PT and TT were in the normal range (not shown). As can be observed, the screening failed to detect abnormalities of the known proteins whose deficiency or abnormality is commonly associated with familial or juvenile thrombophilia.

Very high levels of HRG antigen were consistently demonstrated over a 3-year period in several members of the two families (Table II). In both the families, thrombotic symptoms were exclusively present in members with high levels of HRG. HRG level was also established in 20 children aged 10-15 and found to be significantly lower than adults (mean  $ 83 \pm 20\% $  vs  $ 103.5 \pm 18\% $ ; P < 0.001). This allowed the inclusion among the affected members also of IV.3, aged 14, and IV.4, aged 12, in family B. ## DISCUSSION

Very high levels of plasma HRG were found in six relatives of a patient died at age 34 for massive pulmonary embolism after DVT (Family B, Figure 1) and in the proposita and three relatives of family M (Figure 2). The mother, a maternal aunt and the maternal grandmother of the propositus of family B had suffered from juvenile episode of DVT and several episodes of SVT. The mother also had very high levels of HRG. The two sisters, the brother and two nephews of the propositus showed the same laboratory abnormality without evidence of thrombotic symptoms. In all the subjects carrying the laboratory abnormality, elevated levels of HRG were stable over a 3-year period of study. An autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance of the laboratory abnormality is thus evident. Moreover, thrombosis was evident only in patients with high levels of HRG.

Interestingly, the propositus reported by Engesser et al (4) suffered from myocardial infarction, at age 53, albeit he was still on warfarin therapy for a previous episode of pulmonary embolism. In the propositus of family B, at autopsy, it was possible to ascertain a recent postero-septal myocardial infarction. No other risk factors for arterial thrombosis (dyslipidemia, diabetes, smoke, hypertension) were evident. An additional patient suffering from angina pectoris and with

familial elevation of HRG has been recently described (6). Thus, the association of severe thromboembolic events with arterial thrombosis could be a peculiar feature of this abnormality; however, other families should be reported to assess the true significance of this finding.

Falkon et al. (7) reported a family with high levels of HRG with thromboembolism present only in the proposita, in whom high levels of PAI-1 activity were also evident. An additional family has been recently reported in abstract form (8), with thrombosis in members with elevated levels of HRG and PAI-1. In our patients, PAI-1 activity was in the normal range.

HRG levels are higher in adults than in children (9) and thus it is important to establish the normal ranges according to age. In fact, in our families it was possible to include two nephews of the propositus of family B by adopting a separate range for children.

Apart from mild liver cirrhosis (10), acquired high levels of HRG have not been documented in clinical situations, whereas, on the contrary, low levels have been demonstrated in several conditions, especially sepsis or liver failure (11-13). None of our subjects had clinical or laboratory evidence of liver impairment. HRG is able to lower the amount of free plasminogen and its reduction, as demonstrated by Jespersen et al. (14) in women taking oral contraceptives with low estrogen content, induces an increase of free plasminogen thus enabling an increased binding rate of plasminogen to fibrin. Very high levels of HRG thus could complex large amounts of plasminogen and critically depress fibrinolysis, also considering its release by platelets, following thrombin stimulation, in sites where fibrin formation is ongoing (3).

The reason for the presence in the same family of asymptomatic members who experienced situations at risk, such as pregnancy and surgical interventions, without developing symptoms and affected subjects with severe thrombotic symptoms is not clear but this pattern (low penetrance) has been reported for other families with inherited hemostatic abnormalities predisposing to thrombosis (15).

Following the demonstration of the existence of families with genetically transmitted high levels of HRG, prophylaxis of rethrombosis and prevention in situation at risk should be considered.
