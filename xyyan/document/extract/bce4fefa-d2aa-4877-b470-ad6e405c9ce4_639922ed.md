## A B S T R A C T

This study describes how the serum protein histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) affects the complement system. We show that HRG binds strongly to several complement proteins: C1q, factor H and C4b-binding protein and that it is found complexed with these proteins in human sera and synovial fluids of rheumatoid arthritis patients. HRG also binds C8 and to a lesser extent mannose-binding lectin, C4 and C3. However, HRG alone neither activates nor inhibits complement. Both HRG and C1q bind to necrotic cells and increase their phagocytosis. We found that C1q competes weakly with HRG for binding to necrotic cells whilst HRG does not compete with C1q. Furthermore, HRG enhances complement activation on necrotic cells measured as deposition of C3b. We show that HRG inhibits the formation of immune complexes of ovalbumin/anti-ovalbumin, whilst the reverse holds for C1q. Immune complexes formed in the presence of HRG show enhanced complement activation, whilst those formed in the presence of C1q show diminished complement activation. Taken together, HRG may assist in the maintenance of normal immune function by mediating the clearance of necrotic material, inhibiting the formation of insoluble immune complexes and enhancing their ability to activate complement, resulting in faster clearance.

### 1. Introduction

The plasma protein histidine-rich glycoprotein (HRG) is known to interact with components of the immune system. Although many studies have been published, there is so far no clear understanding of what effect these interactions have on immune function. HRG is a multi-domain 67 kDa protein (Haeberli, 1992) that is present in human plasma at concentrations between 100 and 150  $ \mu $ g/mL (Jones et al., 2005a). As the name suggests, the protein contains an unusually high number of solvent-exposed histidine (and also pro-lines residues) that are concentrated in central  $ \alpha $ -helical domains. The histidine side-chains are involved in the coordination of  $ Zn^{2+} $  ions (Jones et al., 2005a). It is thought that metal binding (and also pH change) causes a conformational change that can affect ligand binding, possibly regulating the biological activity of HRG (Jones et al., 2005a).

Many of the in vivo ligands of HRG are potentially important for modulation of immune system function. For example, the  $ F_{ab} $  region of immunoglobulin G (IgG) molecules have been shown to bind the N-terminal domain of HRG (Gorgani et al., 1999). Additional in vitro experiments showed that the formation of insoluble immune complexes (ILCs) is partially inhibited at physiologically relevant (i.e.  $ 150 \mu g/mL $ ) concentrations of HRG (Gorgani et al., 1997).

An interaction of HRG with C1q, the initiator of the classical pathway of complement, has been documented (Gorgani et al., 1997). However, the consequences of this specific interaction on the downstream activation of complement have not been studied. Complement is part of the innate immune system and consists of three paths of proteolytic cascades that can be activated by: pathogenic microorganism surfaces (lectin pathway), immune complexes (classical pathway) and the autoactivation of the structurally unstable C3 (alternative pathway). The three pathways converge and ultimately lead to the formation of a lytic membrane attack complex (MAC). Complement activation also results

in opsonisation of "self" cells and tissue such as dying cells, which become targeted for phagocytosis (Smith et al., 2002). Additionally, the products of two of the proteolysis reactions are the anaphylaxis and C3a and C5a, which trigger an inflammatory response and assist in recruiting phagocytic cells to sites of injury (Kohl, 2001). In a disease setting, proteins, such as HRG, may trigger elevated complement activation. This could ultimately lead to the establishment of a vicious circle of inflammation, cell death, the delayed clearance of cell debris, continued complement activation, and the eventual destruction of "self" tissue (Jancar and Sanchez Crespo, 2005). Additionally, when immune complexes are not cleared efficiently, they can continue to aggregate and their eventual precipitation is toxic to cells (Charlesworth et al., 1982). Only one report has investigated the link between HRG and complement in detail. It proposes a biphasic modulation by HRG of cascade activation, possibly via interactions with C8, C9, S protein and factor D (Chang et al., 1992). More work is needed to clarify the mechanistic details and consequences of the interaction of HRG with these and other complement proteins.

Also relevant to immune system function, HRG has been reported to bind to necrotic cells via the N1N2 domain and an, as yet, unidentified ligand of cytoplasmic origin (Jones et al., 2005b). As a consequence of HRG binding, enhanced phagocytosis of necrotic cells by macrophages was reported in vitro (Jones et al., 2005b). HRG was also found to bind to late apoptotic cells via DNA and Fcγ-receptor I on mature human monocyte-derived macrophages, which resulted in enhanced phagocytosis (Gorgani et al., 2002). Thus, through opsonisation, HRG may play a physiological role in the clearance of late apoptotic and necrotic cells by phagocytes. Interestingly, in addition to its role as the initiator of the classical pathway (Gaboriaud et al., 2004), C1q binds to apoptotic and necrotic cells (Edward et al., 2005) enhancing their rate of clearance by phagocytic cells. Little is known about the effects HRG may have on this aspect of C1q function. Additionally, because HRG, C1q and IgGs have been shown to bind to one another, a tripartite “interplay” may exist, which could modulate both complement function and immune complex (IC) growth and their complement-activating potential.

The aims of the present study are to: (1) identify HRG-complement interactions, and determine whether these interactions occur in vivo. (2) Continue the investigation regarding what effect HRG may have on complement function and attempt to pinpoint where, and on which pathway, HRG acts. (3) Investigate how the binding of HRG to C1q may affect the interaction of both proteins with necrotic cells, and also the formation and complement-activating ability of insoluble immune complexes.

### 2. Materials and methods

#### 2.1. Materials

Samples of plasma and synovial fluid were collected from rheumatoid arthritis patients at the Department of Rheumatology, Lund University Hospital. Samples were centrifuged and stored at  $ -80^{\circ} $ C until analysis. All patients, n = 20, fulfilled the American College of Rheumatology 1987 criteria for rheumatoid arthritis (Arnett et al., 1988). Pooled normal human serum (NHS) was prepared from the freshly drawn blood of healthy volunteers. The ethical review board of Lund University has approved collection of blood and synovial fluids. C1q was purified from human serum (Tenner et al., 1981). The partial digestion of C1q using pepsin and collagenase (Worthington) was used to prepare tail (Paques et al., 1979) and head (Reid and Porter, 1976) regions. Factor H (FH), factor I (FI) and C4b-binding protein (C4BP) were purified from human plasma according to Blom et al. (2003a), Crossley and Porter (1980) and Dahlbäck (1983), respectively. C3-met and C4-met were prepared as described in Blom et al. (2003c). Mannose-binding lectin (MBL) was purchased from Statens Serum Institut, Denmark. C-reactive protein (CRP) was from Calbiochem, IgG from Immuno and an anti-C1q monoclonal antibody (A201) was from Quidel. C8 and C9 were from Complement Technology, Inc. Chicken ovalbumin was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Fibromodulin was prepared as described in Heinegård et al. (1986). An anti-HRG monoclonal antibody (mAb, HRG-5, G-14-3-4D5/65) was provided by Dr C Parish, Australian National University, Australia and anti-HRG polyclonal antibody (pAb, GHH 20 3) by Dr A Schmidtchen, Biomedical Centre, Lund University, Sweden. Polyclonal anti-factor H (FH) and anti-chicken ovalbumin antibodies were from AbCam. Anti-Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpes virus (KSHV) complement control protein (KCP) and C4BP (PK 8604) pAbs were prepared in-house. The antibodies aE1195B and 9C4-RQ-B were from Dr TE Mollnes, The National Hospital, Oslo, Norway. All other antibodies, and a horseradish peroxidase streptavidin conjugate were from Dako. Fresh sheep erythrocytes were purchased from Swedish Veterinary Institute and Jurkat T-cells were from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC).

#### 2.2. Purification of HRG

Human HRG was purified from defatted plasma by Ni-NTA (Qiagen) affinity chromatography. Plasma was loaded onto a column pre-equilibrated in TBS, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 100  $ \mu $ g/mL PMSF and 2  $ \mu $ g/mL aprotinin. Weakly bound material was eluted using the same buffer with 150 mM imidazole and HRG was eluted using the first buffer with 500 mM imidazole. Pure fractions were dialysed against TBS, pH 7.4, concentrated, by stirred cell ultrafiltration to approximately 1 mg/mL, then frozen at  $ -80^{\circ} $ C.

#### 2.3. Quantitation of concentrations of HRG in serum and synovial fluid

Determinations were made by ELISA. A standard curve was prepared using a serum pool from healthy volunteers. Microtitre plate (Nunc Maxisorp) wells were coated overnight at  $ 4^{\circ} $ C, using  $ 50 \mu L $  of anti-HRG ( $ \alpha $ HRG) pAb, diluted with coating buffer ( $ 75 mM Na_{2}CO_{3} $ , pH 9.6), then washed 3 times (TBS,  $ 2 mm CaCl_{2} $ , 0.1% Tween 20), as was done after all incubation steps. Plates were then blocked ( $ 200 \mu L $  of 1% BSA in washing solution) at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequent incubations were, unless stated otherwise, made at room temperature for 1 h using  $ 50 \mu L $  volumes. Plates were incubated with parallel plasma and synovial fluid samples, each from 20 rheumatoid arthritis patients, diluted in binding buffer ( $ 50 mM $  HEPES, pH 7.4,  $ 100 mM NaCl $ ,  $ 2 mM CaCl_{2} $ ). Next, plates were incubated with a  $ \alpha $ HRG mAb, and then a goat- $ \alpha $ -mouse horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate (30 min incubation), both diluted in blocking solution. Bound HRG was detected using 1,2-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (OPD)/ $ H_{2}O_{2} $  (Dako). The reaction was stopped using  $ 0.5 M H_{2}SO_{4} $  and  $ A_{490} $  values determined.

#### 2.4. Binding of HRG to complement-related ligands

Details as per the above ELISA protocol. Microtitre plate wells were coated overnight with: C1q, FH, C4-met, C3-met, FI, C4BP, IgG, CRP, C8, C9, MBL, fibromodulin and BSA at 6  $ \mu $ g/mL. Following blocking, the plates were incubated with HRG diluted with binding buffer to 1–20  $ \mu $ g/mL. Bound HRG was detected as above. The effect of NaCl on the binding of HRG to C1q and proteolytically cleaved C1q heads and tails was also examined. C1q species were coated overnight at 10  $ \mu $ g/mL. Plates were then incubated with 10  $ \mu $ g/mL HRG diluted using various ratios of binding buffer and 4 M NaCl to achieve NaCl concentrations between 100 and 850 mM.

#### 2.5. Identification of HRG in complex with other proteins in sera

An immune precipitation approach was used. Initially, serum or synovial fluid (100  $ \mu $ L aliquots) was pre-cleared by incubating with an irrelevant  $ \alpha $ FI pAb (20  $ \mu $ g) at 4  $ {}^{\circ} $ C (the incubation temperature for all subsequent steps) for 60 min. Any protein/Ig complexes formed were removed by adding 4  $ \mu $ L protein A Sepharose resin (Sigma) mixed with 16  $ \mu $ L Sephacryl S-100 resin (GE Healthcare) and incubating, with rotating, for 60 min. Subsequently, 20  $ \mu $ g of the following pAbs were added to 100  $ \mu $ L aliquots of fresh sera:  $ \alpha $ HRG,  $ \alpha $ C1q,  $ \alpha $ FH,  $ \alpha $ C4BP,  $ \alpha $ KCP, followed by overnight incubation, with rocking. Fresh protein A Sepharose (as above) was then added followed by incubation, with rotating, for 60 min. The resin was sedimented, retained, washed 3 times in 400  $ \mu $ L cold microtitre plate washing solution (see above), and re-suspended in 45  $ \mu $ L SDS-PAGE sample buffer. Precipitated material was analysed by Western blot using a  $ \alpha $ HRG mAb, then a goat- $ \alpha $ -mouse pAb HRP conjugate. Protein bands were visualized using a luminol enhanced chemiluminescence substrate system (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri).

The binding of HRG from serum to C1q, FH, IgG and C8 was also investigated. HRG ligands were immobilised at  $ 10 \mu g/mL $ , then incubated at  $ 37^{\circ}C $  for 60 min with NHS, diluted 1:10 in binding buffer. Bound HRG was detected using the  $ \alpha $ HRG pAb.

#### 2.6. Direct activation of complement by immobilised HRG

Microtitre plate wells were coated overnight with HRG, IgG, or BSA at  $ 5 \mu g/mL $ . Following blocking, plates were incubated with normal human serum (NHS), diluted in GVB++ (5 mM veronal buffer, pH 7.35, 144 mM NaCl, 0.1% gelatin, 1 mM  $ MgCl_{2} $ , 0.15 mM  $ CaCl_{2} $ ) as follows: deposition of C3d, 1:200 and C5b–C9, 1:10. Serum incubations were done at  $ 37^{\circ}C $  for 20 min (C3d) or 40 min (C5b–C9). For detection, plates were incubated with pAbs against C3d and C5b–C9, then a swine- $ \alpha $ -rabbit pAb HRP conjugate.

#### 2.7. Inhibition of complement activation by HRG

Microtitre plate wells were coated overnight with fibromodulin at  $ 10 \mu g/mL $ , then blocked. NHS was mixed with HRG and GVB++ to achieve a final serum dilution of 1:50 and HRG between 2.5 and  $ 25 \mu g/mL $ . The volume of HRG solution, relative to GVB++ and serum never exceeded 7%. These serum/HRG mixtures were incubated at  $ 4^{\circ}C $  for 1 h before being used in complement deposition assays (see above).

#### 2.8. Effect of HRG on complement-induced erythrocyte haemolysis

Sheep erythrocytes were washed 3 times in DGVB++ (2.5 mM veronal buffer, pH 7.35, 72 mM NaCl, 0.1% gelatin, 2.5% D-glucose, 1 mM  $ MgCl_{2} $ , 0.15 mM  $ CaCl_{2} $ ), then adjusted to  $ 1 \times 10^{9} $  cells/mL. Next, the cells were mixed with an equivalent volume of amboceptor (Dade Behring), previously diluted 1:1500 in DGVB++, and incubated at  $ 37^{\circ}C $  for 20 min. The erythrocytes were washed twice, then re-suspended to  $ 5 \times 10^{8} $  cells/mL. For haemolytic assays, erythrocytes were diluted to  $ 8.6 \times 10^{7} $  cells/mL in DGVB++, then effectors added: HRG (to 0.340  $ \mu $ M), C1q (to 0.056  $ \mu $ M), FH (to 0.103  $ \mu $ M) and C4BP (to 0.038  $ \mu $ M). When two proteins were added simultaneously, they were pre-incubated at  $ 4^{\circ}C $  for 5 min. Haemolysis was initiated by adding NHS, to a final dilution of 1:270 and the process followed, at  $ 37^{\circ}C $ , by measuring the decrease in light scattering ( $ A_{700} $ ). For HRG alone, the protein was also pre-incubated with NHS ( $ 37^{\circ}C $ , 10 min) before being added to the erythrocytes.

Pre-mixed HRG and C1q were also added to C1q-depleted serum, which was then used for haemolytic assays. HRG (554 pmol) and C1q (45 pmol) were pre-incubated for 30 min at 4°C, then added to C1q-depleted serum to give 80  $ \mu $ g/mL C1q and 125  $ \mu $ g/mL additional HRG. The repleted serum was then diluted 1:80, and 25  $ \mu $ L was added to 125  $ \mu $ L sheep erythrocytes at 8.6×10 $ ^{7} $  cells/mL in DGVB++. Extent of haemolysis was assessed spectrophotometrically from amounts of liberated haemoglobin ( $ A_{405} $ ).

#### 2.9. Competition between HRG and C1q for binding to necrotic cells

A flow cytometry approach was employed. Jurkat T-cells (ATCC) in RPMI without serum at  $ 2 \times 10^{6} $  cells/mL were heat-treated at  $ 56^{\circ} $ C for 30 min to induce necrosis. Cells were then washed in flow cytometry binding buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM  $ MgCl_{2} $ , 2 mM  $ CaCl_{2} $ ) and re-suspended to  $ 1.5 \times 10^{7} $  cells/mL. For titration of HRG and C1q with necrotic cells, protein and cells were incubated together at  $ 37^{\circ} $ C for 30 min with gentle shaking. Following washing in flow cytometry binding buffer, the cells were incubated (30 min, room temperature) with a 1:1000 dilution of a  $ \alpha $ HRG mAb or a  $ \alpha $ C1q pAb, washed again, then incubated with a 1:40 dilution of a goat- $ \alpha $ -mouse or swine- $ \alpha $ -rabbit pAb FITC conjugate. The cells were washed and re-suspended in flow cytometry analysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 30 mM  $ NaN_{3} $ , 1% BSA) and the quantities of bound HRG or C1q determined.

For competition studies, C1q labelled with AF488 (Molecular Probes) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and unlabelled HRG, or alternatively unlabelled C1q and AF488-HRG were mixed and pre-incubated at  $ 4^{\circ} $ C for 30 min. Necrotic Jurkat T-cells cells were added and incubated with the proteins at  $ 37^{\circ} $ C for 30 min with gentle shaking. Following washing with flow cytometry binding buffer, the cells were re-suspended in flow cytometry analysis buffer and the quantities of the bound labelled proteins determined. The mole ratios AF488-C1q:HRG of 1:0, 1:1, 1:2 and 1:4, correspond to  $ 7 \mu g/mL $  C1q:0, 1:2, 4.2  $ \mu g/mL $  HRG. For AF488-HRG:C1q, the mole ratios 1:0, 1:0.33, 1:0.66, 1:1 and 1:2, correspond to  $ 15 \mu g/mL $  HRG:0, 1:5, 33.3, 66.6, 100 and 200  $ \mu g/mL $  C1q.

#### 2.10. Confocal microscopy

Necrosis was induced in RPMI without FCS as above. After washing the cells twice with binding buffer (10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM  $ MgCl_{2} $ , 2 mM  $ CaCl_{2} $ ), 1 × 10⁶ cells were incubated with a combination of 350 μg/ml C1q and 50 μg/mL HRG (equimolar amounts) for 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were transferred to ice and washed twice with ice-cold binding buffer and spun for 5 min at 500 rpm on SuperFrostPlus object slides (Menzel, Germany) using a Cellspin (Tharmac, Germany). After fixation with 3.7% PFA for 15 min at room temperature, the cells were blocked for 30 min with 1% BSA in PBS. The cells were next incubated with rabbit α-human HRG and mouse α-human C1q over-night at 4 °C, then with AF488-labelled goat anti-rabbit and AF647-labelled goat anti-mouse, as appropriate, for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, 100 ng/mL propidium iodine (PI) was added and the cells incubated for 15 min at room temperature. In between each stop, the slides were washed extensively with PBS, then dipped briefly in 99% ethanol. After complete drying, the samples were mounted with Dako Cytomation Fluorescent Mounting Medium (Dako) and sealed with nail polish. The slides were stored at 4 °C.

#### 2.11. Complement activation by necrotic cells in the presence of HRG

Necrotic Jurkat T-cells were prepared as described above, resuspended to  $ 1.5 \times 10^{7} $  cells/mL, then incubated with an equivalent volume of  $ 250 \mu g/mL $  HRG in binding buffer. After washing, as above, the cells were incubated with 0.25–5.0% NHS in GVB++

at  $ 37^{\circ} $ C for 30 min. The cells were washed again and incubated (30 min, room temperature) with a 1:200 dilution of a  $ \alpha $ C3c-FITC pAb, washed again, re-suspended in flow cytometry analysis buffer and the quantities of deposited C3b determined using flow cytometry.

#### 2.12. Effect of HRG and C1q on the formation of insoluble immune complexes

The rate of growth of ovalbumin/ $ \alpha $ -ovalbumin IICs was followed, spectrophotometrically, in the presence or absence of HRG and C1q as described (Easterbrook-Smith et al., 1988). All time-course measurements were made in a quartz cuvette at  $ 37^{\circ} $ C in filtered PBS, pH 7.4. Anti-ovalbumin was diluted to  $ 200 \mu $ g/mL in the presence or absence of  $ 125 \mu $ g/mL HRG, 15 or  $ 30 \mu $ g/mL C1q and the systems left to equilibrate for 5 min. Reactions were initiated by adding freshly dissolved ovalbumin, to  $ 10 \mu $ g/mL and the increase in light scatter was monitored ( $ A_{350} $ ). The final reaction volume was  $ 80 \mu $ L and the molar ratio ovalbumin: $ \alpha $ -ovalbumin of 1:6 worked best.

#### 2.13. Activation of complement by insoluble immune complexes formed in the presence of HRG and C1q

The abilities of IICs formed in the presence or absence of HRG or C1q to activate complement were assessed by measuring concentrations of the Terminal Complement Complex (TCC) using ELISA (Mollnes et al., 1985). IICs were prepared fresh, as above, by mixing  $ 200 \mu g/mL \alpha $ -ovalbumin and  $ 10 \mu g/mL $  ovalbumin with 0, 125, 250 and  $ 500 \mu g/mL $  HRG, or 0, 15, 30 and  $ 60 \mu g/mL $  C1q, or both  $ 250 \mu g/mL $  HRG and  $ 30 \mu g/mL $  C1q, or both  $ 125 \mu g/mL $  HRG and  $ 100 \mu g/mL $  C1q. The IICs were then diluted 1/25 in GVB++ containing NHS to a final dilution of 1/25. These samples were incubated at  $ 37^{\circ}C $  for 40 min. To assay for formation of the TCC, the incubations were diluted 1/25 with AG buffer (PBS, 0.02% NaN $ _{3} $ , 0.2% Tween 20, 20 mM EDTA) and  $ 50 \mu L $  dispensed into microtitre plate wells and incubated on ice for 60 min. Microtitre plate wells had been previously coated overnight at room temperature with a 1/10 000 dilution of the Ab aE1195B (Mollnes, 1985) in PBS/0.02% NaN $ _{3} $ . Following washing with PBS, 0.2% Tween 20 (PBST, used after all incubation steps), wells were incubated with  $ 50 \mu L $  of a 1/5000 dilution (in PBST) of the biotinylated Ab 9C4-RQ-B (Mollnes, 1985) at  $ 37^{\circ}C $  for 45 min, then with  $ 100 \mu L $  of a streptavidin/HRP conjugate (Dako) at  $ 37^{\circ}C $  for 60 min. Bound TCC was detected using the Dako OPD colourimetric assay system. A standard curve of zymosan activated serum (where the TCC concentration was defined as 1000 arbitrary units per mL, AU/mL) was used (Mollnes, 1985). The final TCC quantities in samples were expressed as AU/mL.

### 3. Results

#### 3.1. Determination of HRG concentrations in the synovial fluid of rheumatoid arthritis patients

A possible link between HRG and complement was identified following the observation that HRG was one of the most abundant proteins purified from the synovial fluid of rheumatoid arthritis patients by affinity chromatography using C1q immobilised on Affi-gel (BioRad) (data not shown). HRG concentrations in both the plasma and synovial fluid of these patients were determined by ELISA. Fig. 1 shows that the concentration of HRG in plasma  $ (199 \pm 67 \mu g/mL) $  was significantly higher than that in synovial fluid  $ (120 \pm 62 \mu g/mL) $ . Furthermore, there was only a moderate correlation between these two values for each patient  $ (\rho = 0.4466, p = 0.0484) $ . This would be consistent with the filtration of HRG into synovial fluid from plasma, but does not rule out that there is also some local production in the joint.

#### 3.2. Binding of HRG to complement-related ligands

Fig. 2A shows that HRG was successfully purified to homogeneity from human plasma and its identity confirmed by detection with a specific antibody. Coomassie staining showed a trace of aggregated material in the preparation. No attempt was made to remove this material, given its lower abundance relative to the faster-moving HRG band. Material purified as shown was used for all subsequent studies.

The interaction between HRG and purified complement proteins was examined. Immobilised complement proteins were incubated with soluble HRG so that all binding interactions could be assessed using the same  $ \alpha $ HRG mAb. HRG showed selective and strong binding to IgG and the following complement proteins: C1q, FH, C8 and C4BP (Fig. 2B and C). For 6  $ \mu $ g/mL immobilised ligand (assuming complete immobilisation) and 20  $ \mu $ g/mL soluble HRG (assuming complete binding), this represents molar ratios of C1q:HRG (1:24), FH:HRG (1:8), C8:HRG (1:8), IgG:HRG (1:8) and C4BP:HRG (1:29). Binding to C4, C3 and MBL, was also observed. No binding of HRG to FI, CRP, C9, BSA or fibromodulin was seen. With the exception of C8, all of the curves in Fig. 2 reach a plateau, suggesting that maximal binding of HRG to the corresponding ligands had been achieved at 10  $ \mu $ g/mL HRG.

The binding of HRG to isolated head and tail regions of C1q was also examined. Fig. 2D demonstrates that HRG bound mainly to the tail region and that this apparent affinity was essentially the same as that for the intact C1q molecule. The interaction between C1q and HRG also exhibited a moderate dependence on salt concentration. This suggested that ionic as well as hydrophobic interactions were involved in maintaining the interaction between HRG and C1q.

#### 3.3. Identification of HRG in complex with other proteins in sera

Having established that HRG binds strongly and selectively to some complement proteins, we investigated whether HRG is complexed with C1q, FH and C4BP in vivo. Sera from healthy controls and synovial fluid from rheumatoid arthritis patients were incubated with pAbs against HRG (positive control), C1q, FH, C4BP and also KCP (negative control). Immunoprecipitation of these ligands followed by Western blot for HRG revealed that C1q, FH and C4BP were found in complex with HRG in both NHS and the synovial fluid (Fig. 3). No HRG was detected in control samples treated with TBS or immunoprecipitated using an irrelevant control pAb to KCP, demonstrating specific interactions with the above ligands.

#### 3.4. Direct activation and inhibition of complement by HRG

Given that HRG was found to bind to complement proteins in vivo, the possibility that these interactions modulate complement function was investigated. First, the activation of complement by HRG was studied. Immobilised HRG was incubated with NHS and the deposition of mid (C3d) and late (C5b–9) complement cascade reaction products were assessed. In contrast to activation and substantial complement product deposition by aggregated human IgG, no deposition, and thus complement activation, was seen for HRG (Fig. 4A). Thus, HRG alone appeared incapable of activating any of the three pathways of complement.

Next, we investigated whether HRG could inhibit the fibromodulin-induced activation of the classical and alternative pathways of complement (Sjöberg et al., 2005). Fibromodulin was used here because it does not bind to HRG, in contrast to IgG (Fig. 2). If aggregated IgG had been used to activate complement, data would reflect binding phenomena rather than deposition of complement activation products. Fig. 3B indicates that HRG, up to  $ 25 \mu g/mL $ , did not inhibit the deposition of C4b, C3b or C5b–9. At this concentration, the binding of HRG to most complement proteins should have attained saturation (see Fig. 2). Unchanged quantities of deposited C3b and C5b–9 suggested that HRG exerted no inhibitory effect on the classical and alternative pathways. HRG also failed to affect quantities of C3b deposited as a consequence of lectin pathway activation by mannan (data not shown). Thus, it appears that HRG exerted no inhibitory effect on any of the complement pathways.

#### 3.5. Effect of HRG on complement-induced erythrocyte haemolysis

The effect of HRG on the ability of NHS to lyse sheep erythrocytes was examined. These investigations were conducted to corroborate the above results. Fig. 5A indicates that raising the HRG concentration in the haemolytic assay reaction volume by 0.34  $ \mu $ M had no effect on the rate of complement-induced haemolysis. This was true when HRG and serum were added directly to the erythrocytes without any pre-incubation. Additionally, no effect was seen when the HRG had been pre-incubated with the serum for 10 min at 37  $ {}^{\circ} $ C before being added to the cells. Fig. 5A therefore demonstrates that HRG alone exerted no modulatory effect on the rate of complement-induced erythrocyte haemolysis.

We next explored whether the binding of HRG to C1q, FH and C4BP affected complement-induced erythrocyte haemolysis. Adding  $ 0.056 \mu M $  C1q to the assay reaction volume attenuated the rate of erythrocyte haemolysis (Fig. 5B). This suggested that the added C1q had bound to the opsonised erythrocytes, reducing the quantity of IgG available to bind active C1, which could in turn activate complement. When  $ 0.056 \mu M $  C1q and  $ 0.34 \mu M $  HRG were added together, the observed rate of haemolysis was equivalent to that seen for added C1q alone (Fig. 5B). This suggested that the presence of HRG did not affect the ligand recognition functions of C1q. For addition of the complement inhibitors FH (an increase of  $ 0.103 \mu M $ ) and C4BP (an increase of  $ 0.038 \mu M $ ) slower rates of haemolysis were observed (Fig. 5C and D, respectively). When  $ 0.103 \mu M $  FH together with  $ 0.34 \mu M $  HRG and  $ 0.038 \mu M $  C4BP together with  $ 0.34 \mu M $  HRG were added, haemolysis rates were similar to those seen for the addition of only FH or only C4BP.

The effects of the interaction between HRG and C1q were examined further by pre-mixing these two proteins, adding them at physiological concentrations to C1q-depleted serum and using this serum, and a parallel C1q-repleted serum, in haemolytic assays. Rates and extents of haemolysis were the same, irrespective of whether the C1q used to replete the serum had been pre-incubated with HRG (Fig. 5E). Thus, although HRG bound strongly to C1q, FH and C4BP (Fig. 2), these interactions had a negligible effect on the function of these complement proteins.

#### 3.7. Complement activation by necrotic cells in the presence of HRG

Complement is activated by necrotic cells (Ciurana et al., 2004) and the ability of cell-bound HRG to modulate this activation was examined by measuring quantities of deposited C3b. Fig. 6J shows that C3b deposition increased with increasing NHS incubation concentration. #### 3.8. Effect of HRG and C1q on the formation of insoluble immune complexes

As reported earlier by Gorgani et al. (1997) and Easterbrook-Smith et al. (1988), the formation of IICs of ovalbumin and  $ \alpha $ -ovalbumin can be followed over a period of minutes as an increase in light scattering at 350 nm (Fig. 7A). When HRG was added to the system, the physiological concentration of 125  $ \mu $ g/mL, the rate of IIC formation was diminished. Additionally, the final  $ A_{350} $  values attained were significantly lower. This suggests that ovalbumin/ $ \alpha $ -ovalbumin immune complexes formed in the presence of HRG had not attained the size of those formed in the absence of HRG. The reverse was true for C1q. Significantly higher  $ A_{350} $  values were reached at a faster rate. Furthermore, raising the C1q concentration, from 15 to 30  $ \mu $ g/mL, resulted in an approximate doubling of the final  $ A_{350} $  value. Thus, in agreement with the earlier reports (Easterbrook-Smith et al., 1988; Gorgani et al., 1997), C1q appeared to enhance rates and extents of ovalbumin/ $ \alpha $ -ovalbumin IIC formation, whereas HRG exhibited an inhibitory effect.

For IICs formed in the presence of both HRG and C1q, data were similar to those obtained for added C1q alone. Thus, HRG was unable to perturb IIC formation when C1q was present.

### 4. Discussion

The present study gives a deeper insight into the mechanisms by which HRG affects complement and immune function. Interactions of HRG with several components of the immune system have been reported previously. However, detailed information describing the functional consequences of many of these interactions is lacking.

We show here that HRG is present at appreciable concentrations in relevant patient materials, the plasma and synovial fluid of rheumatoid arthritis patients. Therefore, HRG might exert modulatory effects over serum immune function in an autoimmune disease setting. It is thought that HRG is exclusively synthesised in the liver (Koide et al., 1986), although it has also been found in megakaryocytes (Leung et al., 1983). Nonetheless, the presence of HRG in human tissues such as synovial fluid may reflect the acquisition of HRG from plasma (Hulett and Parish, 2000).

We show that HRG binds strongly and selectively to several complement proteins in buffers not supplemented with  $ Zn^{2+} $ . Earlier reports (Gorgani et al., 1997) demonstrated that the binding affinities of HRG for IgG and C1q are affected by  $ Zn^{2+} $ . However, the concentration of free  $ Zn^{2+} $  in vivo is usually very low and tightly regulated (Tubek, 2007), suggesting that under normal conditions, the effect of this cation on HRG binding interactions should not be significant. Additionally,  $ Zn^{2+} $  strongly inhibits complement (Blom et al., 2003b). This would make it impossible to reconcile binding data collected in the presence of  $ Zn^{2+} $  with complement function data.

Having established that HRG binds to complement proteins in vitro, it was important to determine whether these interactions also occur in vivo. In both NHS and synovial fluid samples, HRG co-immunoprecipitates with C1q, FH and C4BP. These are the complement proteins to which HRG binds most strongly in vitro. Furthermore, we demonstrate here that HRG from serum bound to immobilised C1q, FH, C4BP and IgG. We conclude that in vivo at least some HRG is found complexed to the complement proteins C1q, FH and C4BP. It is unlikely that HRG was indiscriminately immunoprecipitated as a consequence of the formation of a ternary complex of HRG/C1q/IgG. We observed minimal immunoprecipitation of HRG when the irrelevant antibody to KCP was used.

Despite the fact that HRG interacts with several complement components it does not appear to affect complement function. Proteins interacting with C1q usually either activate C1q (if they bind to head domains) or inhibit C1q (if the bind to tails) but HRG showed neither effect. Binding to other complement components was also without effect as shown by both ELISA and haemolytic assay. We have noted discrepancies between the present results and those obtained in an earlier study (Chang et al., 1992). In our hands, HRG causes no change in serum-induced erythrocyte haemolysis rates even when pre-incubated with the serum, or when C1q pre-incubated with HRG was used to replete C1q-deficient serum. Earlier, it was suggested that the binding of HRG to C8 inhibits haemolysis and that HRG binds C9 and inhibits polymerization (Chang et al., 1992). However, none of this was reflected in our results.

Here, we confirm that both HRG and C1q bind to necrotic Jurkat T-cells, which are known to increase phagocytosis. Our present data extend these findings by showing that there is only a weak competitive effect between HRG and C1q for binding to necrotic cells. Nonetheless, it seems more plausible that the majority of C1q and HRG interact with different cell surface ligands. C1q and HRG may therefore act in synergy, mediating an accelerated clearance of necrotic material. HRG, when pre-incubated with necrotic cells, also enhanced complement activation and the subsequent deposition of C3b onto the cells. This enhancement could be explained in terms of the binding of serum C1q to cell-associated HRG, initiating a targeted or localised activation of complement. The effect

was only observed at serum concentrations 1% or less, suggesting that the importance of HRG in facilitating the localisation of C1q to necrotic cells becomes less as the concentration of C1q is increased.

Our data indicate that the growth of IICs of  $ \alpha $ -ovalbumin/ovalbumin is inhibited by HRG, but enhanced by C1q, corroborating earlier findings (Gorgani et al., 1997). The outward-facing B chains of the globular C1q heads bind to the interface between the  $ F_{c} $  and  $ F_{ab} $  regions on IgG molecules (Gaboriaud et al., 2004). Thus, C1q could facilitate the accelerated formation of larger IICs by tethering, in a polyvalent manner, ovalbumin/ $ \alpha $ -ovalbumin units via these  $ F_{c}/F_{ab} $  binding sites. HRG, on the other hand, binds via its N-terminal domain to a site in the  $ F_{ab} $  region of IgG molecules (Gorgani et al., 1997). HRG is approximately half the molecular weight of IgG. Therefore, we propose that HRG can inhibit IIC growth by binding to IgG molecules in a monovalent manner, which would introduce steric hindrance effects. This, in turn, would perturb the Fc–Fc interactions that are important for IIC growth (Moller and Bak, 1984). Through steric hindrance effects HRG may also inhibit the binding of multiple IgG molecules to a single antigen molecule, again affecting the rate of IIC growth and final size attainable. When IICs are formed in the presence of both HRG and C1q, the enhancing effect of C1q appears to dominate. Because HRG binds to the tail region of C1q, the globular heads would remain unobstructed and could, hypothetically, still participate in  $ F_{c}-F_{c} $  tethering, thus facilitating the observed enhancement in IIC growth.

Interestingly IICs pre-formed in the presence of C1q show a significantly diminished ability to activate complement. This seems plausible, as the C1q molecules associated with the antigen/antibody matrix would lower the number of free sites on the IgG molecules available to bind soluble C1q, as C1, from the serum. The net effect of this would be to lower the complement-activating potential of the IIC-C1q species.

Most of the former studies on the interaction of ICs with complement have focused on elucidating the mechanism by which serum complement can inhibit IC growth and facilitate IC dissolution. The consensus is that the C1 complex and the early classical pathway act to prevent the formation of very large and IICs (Schifferli et al., 1985) and that C3 cleavage products act to solubilise ICs through their covalent incorporation into the antigen/antibody matrix (Hong et al., 1984; Takata et al., 1984). Our findings, in contrast, describe another scenario, where C1q is directly incorporated into rapidly formed IICs. This incorporation leads to impaired complement activation capacity. Such a scenario has important implications in situations where C1q expression is upregulated, for example in nervous tissue in the latter stages of life and in response to neurodegenerative disorders (Pisalyaput and Tenner, 2008).

We report here that IICs pre-formed in the presence of HRG show a significantly elevated complement-activating potential. Because both HRG and IgG bind C1q, the affinity of soluble C1q for HRG-containing IICs may be greater than that of HRG-free IICs. In this way, the presence of HRG in IICs could facilitate complement activation via enhanced C1q binding. Alternatively, the presence of HRG in or on IICs may result in a change in supramolecular structure, which could hold the relevant binding site on IgG molecules in the antigen/antibody matrix in a more accessible conformation to bind soluble C1q.

For IICs that were pre-formed in the presence of both HRG and C1q, we found that complement activation is attenuated. Thus, the suggested inhibitory effect that is seen for IICs formed in the presence of C1q appears to dominate over any enhancement that is seen for IICs formed with HRG. It remains to be shown whether the incorporation of other serum proteins into immune complexes affects how they activate (and are cleared by) complement.

An earlier study reported that larger soluble immune complexes (SICs) are more efficient activators of the early components of the complement pathway (Doekes et al., 1984) than smaller SICs. However, this does not contradict our results, which show that the smaller IIC–HRGs are better complement activators than the larger IIC–C1qs. An examination of Doekes et al. (1984) indicates that, once SICs reach a size of approximately 4 antigen molecules (and the appropriate number of bound antibody molecules, which is dictated by the molecular weight of the antigen), a further increase in size results in only small increases in complement-activating potential. Because the IIC–HRGs examined in the present study scatter light, they had attained a size significantly larger than those examined in Doekes et al. (1984). Thus, the average size of the IIC–HRGs described here exceeds the size below which diminished complement activation can occur. Therefore, all of the IICs we examine here were large enough that the molecular size effect reported by Doekes et al. (1984) was unlikely to have affected the observed extents of complement activation.

Taken together, it appears that one of the major roles of HRG is to assist in the maintenance of normal immune function. This occurs through: (a) mediating the clearance of necrotic material, (b) preventing ICs from growing too large and insoluble, where they would otherwise become cytotoxic, and (c) increasing the complement-activating potential of ICs, leading to accelerated clearance by serum complement. In the context of autoimmune disorders, we therefore suggest that, over all, HRG acts to counter the effects of immune system dysregulation.

Future studies on HRG could be aimed at developing a therapeutic for autoimmune disorders that could enhance clearance rates of necrotic material, inhibit the formation of IICs and accelerate their complement-mediated removal. Another potentially attractive attribute to HRG is that it does not directly affect complement function, meaning that an HRG-derived therapeutic could be given in conjunction with a second complement-specific therapeutic.
